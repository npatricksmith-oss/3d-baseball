# The approach: fielders slow into the ball and come through it toward the throw (2026-11-20)

**Job.** The owner's Q32 and Q33 (agent-models §5.5, "The catch sets up the throw"): Q33 charged only motion away from the target, because sim fielders reached every ball at full speed and never slowed into it or rounded it, so charging the motion across the line cost about half a run. This job builds the approach (agent-models §5.2): a fielder with time in hand slows into the ball, and an outfielder with time to spare sets up behind a fly and comes through it; then it restores Q32's across term and its drift-accuracy term. Infielders are covered too (owner, 2026-11-20: their throws follow the same mechanics as outfielders'). Step 1 measures the sim as built and finds sources (§1, §3); §2 is why the first set-up behind a fly dropped catches; the spec is agent-models §5.2, §5.3 and §5.5; §5 measures what the change moves. Branch `work/approach`, from main `dce9769`.

Source tags as in the [throw-race note](2026-11-19-throw-race.md): `read` (page text read), `snippet` (search-engine summary only), `unsourced`.

## Summary

- **Sim fielders take a ground ball at a run.** 70% of infield ground-ball takes that lead to a throw, and 66% of outfield pickups, are made at 5 m/s or more, mostly across the line to the throw. An infielder's median time in hand is 0.07 s: he runs at the earliest point he can reach and arrives as the ball does (§1). Real infielders slow into the last steps and field the ball in position (§3).
- **So the across term of Q32 would cost** 0.37 s on a routine grounder, 0.73 s on the charged slow roller, 0.82 s on the backhand in the hole and 0.96 s on an outfield pickup running across (§1).
- **Outfielders who run a ball down going away had time to slow into it**: median 0.80 s in hand at 3–5 m/s away, 0.36 s at 5–7 m/s.
- **The set-up behind a fly dropped catches because its come-through was not timed to the ball** (§2): started from a fixed 2.5 m-from-rest estimate while he was still moving, at a fixed pace toward a point past the catch, the fielder reached the catch point 0.4–1.2 s early, ran on past it, and the ball fell behind him.
- **Sources** (§3): an elite infielder's backhand exchange of 0.60 s at 92% of his arm (Báez 2019, Statcast, read), which the hurry rule reproduces; coaching on slowing into the ball, rounding it or not, the slow roller on the run; no measurement of approach speeds or of an outfielder's pickup-to-release on a ball going away.
- **After the change (§5).** Fielders slow into a ball on the ground; the across term and the drift term are back; the set-up behind a fly comes through without dropping catches. Outfield pickup-to-release 1.08 → 1.05 s (Tango ≈0.95 s; the same rule without the approach 1.46 s), with the plant's knob `PLANT_OVERLAP_S` re-checked and set at 3.2 m/s free. Infield take-to-release unchanged (0.90 s). Triples 1.02 → 0.82 a team-game. Runs 4.27 → 4.39 [4.57 → 4.71]. First to third on singles 33% → 36% (MLB 32%): outfielders run straight at the ball, so a throw to third is across their motion; rounding the ball is not built (proposed owner question Q34, §6). All six frozen plays pass.

## 1. How fielders take a ball they throw (as built, main `dce9769`)

The probe (`zz_ct_probe.rs`, from the catch-throw job) runs direct seeds 0–299 and 1000–1299 (both pooled). "Toward", "across" and "away" are by the larger part of his velocity along or across the line to the target; "standing" under 1 m/s. What it shows:

- **Every fielder takes a ground ball at a run.** 70% of infield ground-ball takes and 66% of outfield pickups are made at 5 m/s or more, and most of that motion is across the line to the throw (infield 3,723 of 9,836 across at 5 m/s+, outfield 2,850 of 8,319). An infielder's median time in hand is 0.07 s: he runs at his earliest intercept, the earliest point of the ball's path he can reach, so he arrives as the ball does, at speed (agent-models §5.2: the intercept plan has him "arriving at speed", and only a chaser early for the ball brakes). Real infielders on a routine grounder slow into the last steps and take it in fielding position (§3).
- **That is why the across term cost so much (Q33).** Charged in full, it would add 0.37 s to the routine grounder, 0.73 s to the charged slow roller, 0.82 s to the backhand in the hole and 0.96 s to an outfield pickup made running across at 5 m/s+ (the mean plant on all outfield pickups 0.17 → 0.56 s; on infield ground balls 0.07 → 0.44 s).
- **Outfielders running a ball down going away had time to slow into it.** At 3–5 m/s away the median time in hand is 0.80 s, at 5–7 m/s 0.36 s: by the braking cost of §4 that is enough to arrive 2–4 m/s slower, which saves 0.2–0.6 s of plant. Those are the triples (catch-throw note §5).
- **Fly catches are mostly made with time in hand** (median 1.1–2.2 s in every class but the fastest), so the across term would cost little there once the set-up and arriving under control apply: 0.22 s on average as caught now, almost all on catches across or away at 5 m/s+ (557 + 298), which are the late ones (0.4 s in hand).
- **The infield cases.** The slow roller is already thrown on the run: 42% of them hurried, 0.55 s from take to release. The backhand is released in 0.64 s with no plant charged now (its motion is across, not away); with the across term in full it would owe 0.82 s or a hurried, off-balance throw, which is what Báez's elite 0.60 s at 92% of his arm looks like (§3).

## 2. The set-up behind a fly: why it dropped catches

The catch-throw job built the set-up behind a fly (`2f39851`, `4ebf724`, `050cc39`) and took it out (`66911ff`): fly-ball hits .097 → .104 and pop-up hits .004 → .016 over 300 games. Its last version was restored on this branch's base for a throwaway probe (`setup-restored-probe.diff`, with an off switch), and `bip_outcomes` found the first ball in play that differed in each of 100 games: 15 games differed: in 12 the set-up turned a fly ball out into a hit (launch 35–54°, an outfielder with a runner on), in 2 a hit into an out, and in 1 the first difference was another ball. Three were traced (`trace_play`, with a log of each set-up decision):

| Play | Fielder | Came through at (ball down by his read) | At the catch point | Where the ball came down |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| seed 53, play 23 | CF | 3.72 s (5.09 s), already moving in at 3.7 m/s | 4.45 s, 0.63 s early, moving in at 3.8 m/s | at 5.18 s, 4.4 m behind him |
| seed 48, play 86 | LF | 4.99 s (6.10 s) | 5.72 s, 0.37 s early | at 6.20 s, 2.6 m behind him |
| seed 11, play 132 | RF | 4.25 s (5.62 s) | 4.45 s, 1.2 s early | at 5.71 s, behind him |

**Why.** The old rule started him through when the time left was a run of 2.5 m from rest at the 4 m/s pace (about 1.3 s with τ = 1 s), whatever he was doing, and sent him at a point 3 m past the catch point at a flat 4 m/s:
- he was seldom standing on his spot: he was still moving in toward it (within the 1 m that counted as there), often at 3–4 m/s already, so the run took a fraction of the time it was budgeted;
- nothing timed the come-through to the ball, so arriving early he ran on past the catch point toward the base, and the ball fell behind him. Once past it his reads put the catch out of reach (an intercept 30 m away on the ground), so he was no longer under it at all;
- on the way to the spot the decision flipped between the spot and the catch point every tick (seed 11, 2.41–2.73 s), because the test for going there and the test for staying differed.

**The fix** (agent-models §5.5 and §5.3): the start is timed from his actual distance to the catch point; coming through, his pace is his distance to his latest read of the catch point over the time left (recomputed every tick, at most his top speed), so he arrives with the ball and never passes the point first; once he has chosen the spot he keeps it while the time allows (no flipping). §5 measures it.

## 4. The design

The rules are in agent-models §5.2 ("The approach to a ball on the ground"), §5.3 (the arrival speed and the come-through pace) and §5.5 (the plant across the line, the drift term, the crow hop's gain on a set throw only, the set-up behind a fly). In short:

- **Slowing into the ball.** A chaser going for a ball he takes on the ground weighs every point of his own predicted path he can reach before the ball, from his intercept to 1.5 s past it: the time in hand at a point lets him arrive slower (from v with s in hand, v − √(2·`BRAKE`·v·s)), and his ready time there is the ball's time plus the plant he would owe at that speed. He takes the earliest ready time. With no time in hand his intercept wins and he goes straight at it, as before.
- **The base he expects to throw to** is read from what everyone sees: an outfielder, the base ahead of the lead runner (second with nobody on); an infielder, second for the double play with a runner on first and fewer than two out, otherwise first.
- **The cases.** The routine grounder: slowed into a later hop. The slow roller: no later point is in time, so on the run and off balance (the hurry rule), with no crow-hop gain. The backhand in the hole: the ball goes away from him, so rarely a later point; the plant or the hurried throw. The gap ball run down going away: slowed into it as it slows, the rest of the stop paid as the plant.
- **Rounding** as a curved run is not built (proposed): coaching is split on it and the plan would need the turn model. The choice of a take point further along a crossing ball does part of it.
- **The set-up behind a fly**, rebuilt with the come-through timed to the ball (§2).
- **Q32 restored:** the plant counts the motion across the line again, and the hurry rule's off-balance terms follow the drift still unbraked at the release (a weight of 0 to 1 in place of the old all-or-nothing).
- **Beliefs.** Each fielder's approach and set-up are his own plan from his own read. The holder's own race uses his plant, drift and gain; runners read the plant from the motion they see at the catch, as before, so the across term reaches them through the same rule; a runner's read of a ball not yet fielded is unchanged (§5 measures the gap).

- **The approach does what it was built for.** Infielders take the routine grounder at 4.3 m/s instead of 5.4. Outfielders who ran a ball down going away now take it slower: the 5–7 m/s class shrank from 602 to 228 takes, and the 3–5 m/s class releases in 0.95 s against 1.18 s. With the across term restored, an outfielder's pickup-to-release is 1.05 s, against 1.46 s for the same rule without the approach and 1.08 s on main.
- **Tango's ≈1.2 s with the tag** (≈0.95 s of release): main 1.08 s, final 1.05 s.
- **The infield is about where it was:** take-to-release 0.90 s on main and on this branch; the take is 0.02 s later, the release 0.025 s later.
- **The slow roller** is now thrown on the run (69% hurried, 0.57 s), as the coaching describes, with no crow-hop gain. **The backhand** is slower to release (0.79 s, 21% hurried): his motion across the line to first now counts, and Báez's elite 0.60 s at 92% of his arm is what the hurry rule gives an elite `Transfer` (§3, test `the_hurry_rule_matches_a_measured_backhand_exchange`).
- **The set-up behind a fly now happens without dropping catches.** A third of the catches with a runner on and 0–1 out are made coming toward the target faster than the crow hop's 3 m/s (main 9%). Their throws are 0.4 m/s harder. With the set-up switched off by a throwaway switch, 2 of 100 games' balls in play differed (one fly a hit, one a hit an out), against 15 of 100 with the first build's set-up, 12 of them dropped flies (§2).
- **Fly catches release 0.05 s later**: catches made running across the line (the late ones) now owe the across plant.

The sim has no fielding errors (the scorer's seam, rules-spec §10.2); dropped flies show in the fly-ball and pop-up hit rates.

**What moved and why:**

- **Triples fall a fifth** (1.02 → 0.82), and doubles rise by about as many: outfielders slow into a ball going away and owe less of the stop. With the knob at 3.2 m/s the plant on those balls is smaller too. Triples are still six times MLB's; the plant was not their main cause (catch-throw note: 0.87–0.89 before the plant).
- **Outfield assists fall** 0.29 → 0.25 (MLB 0.133): fewer outfield throws to third (first throw to third with a runner on first only: 94 → 53 in 600 games). The throws to second come about as fast as before (contact-to-release 5.77 → 5.74 s).
- **First to third on singles rises** 33.4% → 36.4% (MLB 31.7%), and fewer outfielders throw to third. An outfielder fielding a single comes in toward second, so a throw to third is across his motion; it now owes the across plant, so he judges the runner safe and throws to second, and the runner, who sees the same motion, goes. Real outfielders charge such a ball on an angle that lines them up with third ("ball at you, lead runner"; §3). That is rounding, which is not built (§6, Q34). Planning the approach for the lead runner's base only (a throwaway switch, seed 0) made no difference (37.6% against 37.3%), so it is not the choice of base: the run is straight, and braking is all the plan can do about direction.
- **Runs rise 0.12 / 0.14** in the standard runs (probe 0.11 / 0.02). Part is the across plant on throws to third and home (first to third above). Part is noise: on seed 1000, HR/PA moved 2.72% → 2.86%, which no fielding change touches (≈0.05 runs).
- **Tags from third and second** move within each bin's noise, in opposite directions on the two sets.
- **Fly-ball and pop-up hits** are +0.008 / +0.002 on both sets, about 1.5 standard errors. The first version of this build let a fielder slow for a ball still in the air when his read had it landing in front of him, and dropped more (fly .114, pop-up .010, seed 0); the final one goes hard for any ball in the air. The set-up switched off changes 2 balls in 100 games (above), so it is not the set-up. Not otherwise explained.
- **Time per game**: the planner costs 4% (4,735 → 4,909 ms per game per core on seed 0, the CLI's measure). A first version, which timed every candidate take with the reach-time search, cost 22%.

**Frozen plays** (`bb_game/tests/throw_choices.rs`): all six pass. With the across term and no approach, three infield plays failed (catch-throw note §6). The fixes that brought them back: the pivot man's and relay man's exchanges and a toss count only motion away (their sourced times include the footwork; a feed is flipped), the infielder plans for the lead force, and the free speed of the knob.

## 6. Proposed owner question (Q34): rounding the ball

- **What the game does now.** A fielder with time slows into a ball on the ground, so he takes it under control, but he runs straight at the point where he takes it. Coming in on a single to center with a runner on first, he arrives moving toward second; a throw to third is across his motion and owes the plant (the across term, Q32).
- **What is wrong.** Real outfielders come at such a ball on an angle that lines them up with third ("ball at you, lead runner"; infielders "round it, then come through the ball"), so they throw to third with their momentum. Here they give up on third: outfield throws to third with a runner on first fell from 94 to 53 in 600 games, and runners go first to third on singles 36.4% of the time (main 33.4%, MLB 31.7%).
- **Options:**
  1. **(Recommended)** Build rounding: when he has time in hand, the approach also chooses the direction he arrives from (a point behind the take point, away from the base he expects to throw to, then through it toward the base, like the set-up behind a fly), with the curve's extra time in his plan. It needs the plan to know the turn of §5.3 and is a job of its own.
  2. Leave it: a little more first-to-third and runs than MLB until rounding is built.
  3. Count the across part at the cornering limit (`Agility`, 7 m/s²) in place of the braking limit (Q33's option 2): cheaper, but wrong for a man who runs straight across.
- **Recommendation:** 1, as the next fielding job. Until then 2, with this build.

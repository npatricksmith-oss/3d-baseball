## 1. Summary

Today's swing (v2) is one rigid plane laid backward from the contact pose. It meets the ball the way MLB hitters do (Statcast) but before about 40 ms ahead of contact it is a different swing: the bat flat and pointing at the catcher where a measured hitter's is 40° up and wrapped behind his head, in line with the arms where his is cocked 120–130° behind them (diagnosis §3).

v3 keeps what v2 gets right and rebuilds the rest:

- **What moves.** The bat (the only physical body), held at the **hands** (mid-wrists, a point on the handle), whose path is built round a **shoulder point S** (the clavicle, the root of [4]'s arm vector) that follows the body. Three elements; the pelvis, trunk turn, legs and head stay presentation keyed to the sim's clocks (§3).
- **Where it starts.** A **launch pose L** per batter, the same on every pitch, at rest. For the average batter it is [4]'s at weight commitment (−170 ms): the bat 41° up and wrapped (azimuth −225°), cocked well behind the arms ([4]: 121°; 129° as built), the hands just in front of the back shoulder at its height (§6.1). A batter with a longer swing (faster bat, slower hands) starts more wrapped and more upright, a shorter one less (§6.7).
- **How it meets the ball.** The **contact jet**, the pose, velocity and acceleration at t_c, is v2's plan exactly: the sweet spot at the aim point at `BatSpeed`, with Statcast's attack angle, attack direction, path tilt and vertical bat angle, and v2's reach rule (§6.2). After t_c the bat is v2's (§6.4). Before t_c the measured shape brings the bat into the contact window differently from today (§6.5).
- **Between.** From L at rest to [4]'s mid-swing anchor (−93 ms: azimuth 168° short of contact and turning at 56% of its contact rate, elevation 39°; the hands at a per-batter point built from [4]'s arm angles), then [4]'s own measured azimuth rate and elevation (Appendix E, scaled to the jet's contact rate and elevation) into the contact jet; the hands on one smooth curve throughout (§6.3). The lag through the swing is an outcome checked against [4] (121° → 133° max at −80 ms → 65° at contact).
- **Every batter, every effort (D27, §6.7).** The average batter's measured swing, built in its own phase, is the template; a batter's swing is the template scaled: its travel by λ = (`BatSpeed` × his swing time) over the average's (MLB's swing length rises with bat speed in proportion), its time by his `QuickHands` and the swing's effort (an easier swing is the same swing, slower, as a driven chain's is). In the zone every rating and effort at ±3 sd gives a sweet spot that rises into the ball with one surge (at most 1.06× its contact speed).
- **Checked swings** brake by slowing the swing's own clock, so the bat stays on its path; the ruling measures the bat's turn from L (rules-spec §4.5's wording), so a wrapped bat is no longer "a swing" before it moves (§8).
- **Determinism and noise** are as v2: the same five execution draws in the same order on the same two streams; no new RNG purpose (§9).
- **What changes in games**: every swing takes its own time (0.170 s for the average batter on a middle pitch if the owner confirms it, §6.7.7: he decides 20 ms earlier than today; outside pitches take longer, inside ones less, an easy swing longer). Balls met before the planned instant (late swings; 23% of contact is 3 ms or more before it): 25 ms before t_c the bat is 3–4° further back, 6–11° higher, its sweet spot 1 mph slower to 9 mph faster than v2's on the same plan and travelling 8–16° more steeply downward; 10 ms before, under 1° back, under 1.2° higher, 1–2 mph faster and 2–6° more steeply downward; 3 ms before, within 0.5°; at and after t_c it is today's bat (§6.7.6). Checked swings change a lot (the braked bat is far back: about 0.6 fewer strikes a game). The recalibration should be small to moderate (§12).
- **A finding about where the batter stands** (§5.3; decided D26, Q46 = a: he is not moved in this rebuild, a later step if a plate-relative contact depth confirms it): the measured arms at contact ([4]'s shoulder angles and its 65° bat-to-arm angle) put the hands about 0.4 m in front of the batter's chest, toward the pitcher. With today's contact depth and stance spot the sim's batter would have to stand about half a metre further back in the box than he does (at about the same distance from the plate), or meet the ball that much further out in front. Either the sim's batter stands in the wrong place or its contact point is too deep (its depth level, 0.35 m, is unsourced); Statcast's batter-relative intercept settles it. The oracle backs the finding: [4]'s lag curve (falling steadily from 133° to about 60° at contact) appears only with the body 0.55–0.70 m deeper; with today's stance the lag bottoms out at 28° 20 ms before contact (middle aim) and rises again to 51°. Moving him deeper is not free: it also moves the hands' anchors and so the late path, and the bat tip's speed then peaks at 1.13–1.22 times its contact value (against [3]'s 1.07), so it is a game question, not only a look (§5.3).

## 2. What v3 must keep: today's contact (the baseline)

The collision reads one thing from the swing: the bat's pose and velocity field at the contact instant, inside t_c ± 25 ms (physics-spec §7). Nothing else that decides a game reads the bat before t_c − 25 ms except a checked swing (diagnosis §2: the brake, the braked path, the ruling). The fielders read the bat's horizontal angle at t_c and the swing's lead t_plate − t_s (`game.rs:409-415`).

Today, over the standard run's 600 games (77,434 swings; [baseline](calibration/2026-11-24-swing-contact-baseline.md)):

| At contact (full swings, n 43,590) | mean | sd |
| :-- | --: | --: |
| contact − t_c | +1.2 ms | 5.6 |
| sweet-spot speed | 71.2 mph | 4.3 |
| attack angle / direction | 9.1° / +2.2° | 5.9 / 9.2 |
| bat elevation / azimuth ([4]'s convention) | −29.5° / +7.7° | 3.5 / 13.4 |
| bat against its path (hooked +) | +0.27° | 1.52 |
| knob speed / bat angular speed | 6.4 m/s / 2,046°/s | 1.0 / 142 |
| EV, LA, spray (pull +) of all contact | 84.1 mph, 16.4°, −14.2° | 15.9, 30.6, 47.2 |

Slopes: spray +3.2° and bat-against-path +0.27° per ms of contact − t_c; launch angle +10.1° per cm of undercut; EV 1.01 mph per mph of sweet-spot speed. Checked swings: 13.2% of swings, the brake at −82 ± 32 ms, 3.4% ruled swings (310 touches, 36 on the yaw).

**By construction v3 reproduces** every quantity at the planned contact instant (the jet is v2's) and every contact after it (§6.4: the bat is v2's there; 57% of contacts). **It moves** contacts before t_c, which meet a bat further back and faster than today's, by an amount that grows quickly with the timing error (§6.5: under 1° back, 1–1.5° higher and moving 4–8° more steeply downward at −10 ms; 4–7° back and 11–14° higher at −25 ms), and checked swings a lot (§12). It is not "v2 to second order" over the window: the first version of this spec said so, and the review measured otherwise.

## 3. The state: what moves, and why these three

| Element | What it is | Degrees of freedom | Source of its motion |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Bat** | rigid body of revolution (physics-spec §6), the only thing the world reads | 5: direction â (azimuth A, elevation E) and the hands' point W on its axis | [4] angles; the contact jet |
| **Hands** W | the mid-wrists, taken as the point of the bat's axis at u_g = 0.08 m from the knob [E] (between the two hands; the real mid-wrists sit 5–8 cm off the axis, which moves the lag by a few degrees) | (part of the bat's 5) | [4] arm angles at two instants; the contact jet |
| **Shoulder point** S | [4]'s "clavicle", the root of the arm vector a = W − S | 3, prescribed (no dynamics, no decision) | [4] pelvis travel and trunk angles; the stance spot |

Lag λ = ∠(a, â), [4]'s `BatLag()`: 0° = the bat in line with the arms, 180° = pointing back along them ([3] reports the supplement). The arm vector's turn is [3]'s "arms" segment.

**Why not fewer.** Without S there is no arm vector, so no lag: the measured relation of bat to hands, which D25 names, could not be stated or checked. Without a hands path of its own (hands fixed on a circle about one centre, as v2), the hands cannot go from beside the back shoulder at launch to 0.4 m in front of the chest at contact while the bat stays cocked. One plane for the bat (v2) cannot hold the bat 39–44° up while it turns 50° in azimuth ([4] −170 to −93 ms) and then bring it to −30°: no single plane contains that path (diagnosis §1).

**Why not more.** Pelvis, trunk turn, legs and head do not touch the bat once S's path and the hands' path are given, and nothing in the sim reads them. They stay presentation, keyed on the sim's clocks as [player-visuals §13.2](player-visuals.md#132-the-timing-model) designs (the hips and chest peak before the sim's arm and bat). The proximal half of the measured sequence (pelvis 581°/s at −70 ms, trunk 766°/s at −57 ms [4]) is therefore the client's, checked there; the distal half (arm, then bat) is the sim's, checked here (§11). Modelling them in the sim would add state that no outcome reads. If a later model drives the bat from the body (a torque-driven chain), it can replace this one behind the same interface (`BatPose`).

**Elbows** are not sim state. The distance |a| follows from the hands' path; the drawn elbows' angles follow from |a| and the figure's arm lengths and are a check (rear 81°, lead 56° at contact [4]).

## 4. Frames, definitions and time

### 4.1 Frames

The batter's frame, for a right-handed batter (a left-hander is mirrored in x, as now): x̂ toward the plate from the batter, ŷ toward the pitcher, ẑ up. It is the sim frame for a right-hander (AGENTS.md "Units and frames").

### 4.2 Angles ([4]'s definitions, from its Appendix C code)

- **Bat azimuth A and elevation E** (`BatLag()`: `BatPhi − 270`, `90 − BatTheta`): â = (cos E cos A, cos E sin A, sin E). A = 0° the bat across the plate (tip toward the plate), −90° at the catcher, −180° pointing away from the plate behind him, −225° wrapped behind his head toward the pitcher's side. A increases through the swing (counter-clockwise seen from above for a right-hander) and is **unwrapped**: continuous from L, never reduced modulo 360°. The jet's contact azimuth (from `atan2`) is taken on the branch A_L + 180° < A_c ≤ A_L + 540°, that is −45° < A_c ≤ 315° (a bat cannot meet the ball pointing back past the catcher; the planner's contact azimuths run −10° to +24°).
- **Lag λ** (`BatLag()`): the angle between â (knob → cap) and the vector from the clavicle to the mid-wrists, as above.
- **Shoulder elevation θ and azimuth φ** (`PolarShoulder()`): [4]'s code takes the polar angles of the vector **from the elbow to the shoulder** joint centre in the upper trunk's frame, the same formula for both arms: θ = its angle from the trunk's long axis (so 0° = arm hanging, 90° = horizontal), φ = atan2(y, x) − 90° in the upper trunk's own frame (step 0, below). This spec never uses that vector or [4]'s axes directly. It places the **shoulder-to-elbow** unit vector d in its own trunk frame (x_T forward from the chest, y_T toward the lead side, t̂ up the trunk; §5.3):

  **d = −cos θ·t̂ + sin θ·(cos φ·x_T + sin φ·y_T)** (reading 1, **settled by step 0**),

  so φ = 0° is the upper arm pointing forward from the chest and negative φ turns it toward the batter's rear side (the rear arm out to the side and back; the lead arm across the chest). Reading 2, the only other one the code allows, reverses the horizontal part (φ + 180°) and is **ruled out**. *Step 0 (Appendix B, p. 185–186 of the PDF, printed 171–172; Appendix C `PolarShoulder()`, printed 185–188):* the code reads the shoulder and elbow joint centres `V_*_Shoulder_JC_Trunk` and `V_*_Elbow_JC_Trunk`, markers expressed in the upper trunk's own frame, takes shoulder − elbow, θ = acos(Z/|r|) (so Z is the trunk's long axis, up) and φ = atan2(Y, X) − 90°. Appendix B's diagrams draw the angles but label no axes. The axes follow from [4]'s data: the trail arm at launch (elevation 71°, azimuth −78°, elbow out to the side) puts elbow → shoulder along trunk +X with a small +Y, so +X is toward the lead side and, for a right-handed frame with Z up, +Y is **backward**; then azimuth 0° at contact (both arms, −1° and −9°) is the elbow → shoulder vector pointing straight back at the chest, the arms reaching forward, as they must. In the spec's terms this is exactly reading 1 (φ = 0° the upper arm forward, negative φ toward the rear side; forward coordinate of the elbows rising +0.07 → +0.19 m, §11 case 2). Residual doubt: the frame's handedness is inferred, not labelled; the contact check (arms forward, not back) would fail under the mirror image. **No spec number moves** (W_L, W_93, §5.3 unchanged). **The check that told them apart before was [4]'s own prose**: during swing initiation "the trail upper arm dropped and moved forward" while its azimuth rose −78° → −42°, and during acceleration the lead arm went "down and forward" as its azimuth rose −24° → −1°. Under reading 1 the rear elbow's forward coordinate (along x_T, from the rear shoulder) rises +0.07 → +0.19 m from launch to −93 ms and the lead elbow's +0.30 → +0.32 → +0.32 m; under reading 2 both are reversed (the elbows move back). The oracle asserts the sign (§11). Reading 2 would also put the launch hands only 0.15–0.28 m from the clavicle, against the swing-start hands near the back shoulder the sources describe.
- **Elbow and knee**: flexion, 0° straight.

### 4.3 The swing's clock and [4]'s times

*Revised for D27 (§6.7): the time is each swing's own.* The swing starts at **t_s = t_c − T**, at rest, and maps onto [4]'s **weight commitment, −170 ms** ([4] §4.1.3–4.1.4: "swing initiation", when the bat "finally began to rotate forward"; [4]'s bat turns at 240°/s there, 10% of its peak, the same threshold v2 used to define its start). T is the swing's own time (§6.7.3):

**T = T_t × (T_b / T̄) / (1 + e)**,

T_t the average batter's time at this aim (the time in which [4]'s measured rate shape, scaled to the aim's contact rate, covers the aim's sweep from his launch), T_b the batter's own swing time (`QuickHands`), T̄ the average batter's (0.170 s [E: [4]'s commitment-to-contact time; the owner's choice, §6.7.7]) and e the swing's effort draw. [4]'s times are scaled into the swing by **s_T = T / T₄**, T₄ = 0.170 s, so an event [4] puts at τ₄ comes at τ = s_T·τ₄, and [4]'s rates are used only as ratios to the contact rate (§5).

The decision clock follows the batter's own swing time, t_commit = t̂_arrival − T_b − T_latency (agent-models §3.4), and a swing whose own T is longer starts earlier, inside the latency (§6.7.5).

*History.* Step 1 squeezed [4]'s times into a single T_swing of 0.150 s for every batter (D26, Q48 = a) and kept [4]'s angles, which forced the swing's speed and not its time to absorb every difference between batters and efforts (§6.7.1); D27 superseded that.

The bat is **at rest at t_s** ([4]'s bat turns at 236°/s at −170 ms: it has been unwinding slowly since its minimum azimuth at −243 ms, 7° in 73 ms). v3 drops that 236°/s and starts from rest; the gain is a launch pose the client's load can end on exactly, the same on every pitch. The travel the dropped rate would have made is kept: the template covers [4]'s whole sweep from −170 ms (Î, §6.7.3), from rest.

### 5.1 What the sources do not give, and what v3 assumes

Plainly, so no one mistakes an assumption for a measurement:

- **The curves between the stated instants.** [4] states azimuth, elevation and lag at −170, −93, −80 (lag only) and contact; its Appendix E graphs have the full curves, which step 0 digitised (§5.4). Since step 1, v3 uses two of them as data from −93 ms to contact (the azimuth rate as a ratio to its contact value, and the elevation, §6.3) and joins launch to −93 ms with the lowest-order smooth curves [E]. The first version joined the stated values with single polynomials; step 0 found its azimuth confirmed (within 5–8°) and its elevation 8–13° too early between −80 and −25 ms (§5.4), and step 1 replaced it. The shape is not neutral: it is what brings the bat into the contact window faster, further back and higher than today's (§6.5).
- **The hands' position at any instant.** Neither [3] nor [4] gives it. v3 builds it at −170 and −93 ms from [4]'s arm angles on the figure's segment lengths, choosing the forearm's roll by a least-misfit rule (§6.1), and takes it from the contact jet at t_c. Between, it is a smooth curve [E]. The arm angles, the lag and the lead arm's length do not fit exactly together on these proportions (§6.1's residuals).
- **The elevation at launch** (41° [E], between [4]'s 44° and 39°; the probe found 39° and 44° change nothing that matters).
- (Settled in step 0: the trunk frame of [4]'s shoulder azimuth, §4.2, reading 1.)
- **The trunk's lean at launch and its flexion at contact**, and the pelvis's travel in the last phase (§5.3's table, [E] marks).
- **Where the body is relative to the contact point** (§5.3).
- **The hand speed at contact.** [3] and [4] do not give it; v3 keeps v2's (q = 0.32, hands at about 6.4 m/s; [3]'s arm and uncocking rates suggest 6–9 m/s, a check, §11).
- **Anything after contact.** v3's track ends at t_c + 0.03 s (D24); the client draws the follow-through.

### 5.3 The shoulder point S, the trunk frame, and the body at contact

The body is built at three knots, **L** (τ = −T), **93** (τ_93 = −0.093·s_T) and **c** (τ = 0), for a right-hander in the batter's frame (a left-hander is built the same and mirrored in x). #### 6.7.1 What makes a real bat speed up into the ball

A real swing is driven. The legs and trunk turn the shoulders, the arms pull the hands round, and the bat, cocked behind the hands, is pulled after them; as the arms slow (the trunk peaks at −57 ms, the lead arm at −43 ms [4]) the bat's lag is released and the bat turns faster than the arms ever did (uncocking at 1,588°/s at −20 ms [3]). The bat speeds up into the ball because something is still pushing it, and the push and the release come in the same order on every swing. A weaker or an easier swing is the same sequence with less push: every segment is slower, so the same path takes longer.

That is a physical fact, not only a description, and the probe shows it with a driven arm-and-bat chain (a planar double pendulum: a torque at the body's axis, the bat hinged at the hands with a wrist stop holding its cock; `driven_chain_probe.py`, not fitted to [4]). Scaling every torque by f from 0.70 to 1.30 gives the same path in the swing's own phase, the time × 1/√f and every speed × √f: time × contact speed is constant (9.290 m to four figures), the bat's speed rises to one peak 1.049 times its contact value at the same phase of every swing (−15.3 ms at f 0.70, −12.8 at 1.0, −11.3 at 1.30, so at a fixed share of the swing's time). Gravity on the bat (applied fully in the swing's plane, the worst case) breaks the similarity by under 1% (T × v 9.355–9.410 m, peak 1.040–1.044). So **a slower swing by the same batter is the same swing, slower, and it takes longer**; MLB's bat tracking says the same (note 10 §2: at the same contact depth a 4 mph slower swing is 0.7% shorter, so it lasts about 5% longer).

**What the merged path did instead.** Step 1 fitted [4]'s measured shape (angles against time) and then forced every swing into it with fixed numbers: the launch 225° of azimuth behind the ball for everyone, 168° still to turn 82 ms before contact for everyone, 0.150 s for everyone, while the contact speed followed `BatSpeed` and effort. The turn's size and time were fixed and its speed was not, so the measured rate shape could not fit: a slow swing had to turn faster in mid-swing than at the ball (1.24× at three sd slow) and a fast one had to ease off and surge again. No bound can make that realistic; the construction has to put the batter's travel and time where the drive puts them.

**What a model needs for this to come out by construction.** (1) A shape defined in the swing's own phase u = τ/T: angles as shares of the swing's own travel and rates as shares of its own contact rate, so the shape does not depend on how fast the swing is. (2) The travel and the time each following from something: within a batter the travel is fixed (his launch is the same on every pitch, D22, and MLB's slower swing at the same depth is as long) so the time follows the speed, T ∝ 1/(contact speed); across batters MLB's swing length rises in proportion to bat speed (note 10 §1: length ÷ speed flat at 68–70 ms), so the travel follows `BatSpeed` at a common time, and `QuickHands`, a quicker swing at the same speed, is a shorter swing. (3) The whole path scaled together (azimuth, elevation and hands), not one angle: scaling the azimuth alone leaves the elevation's 72° drop and the hands' 0.6 m to be covered in a shorter swing, and a compact batter's sweet spot then overspeeds (1.13× at the middle pitch; §6.7.2, model ii-a).

An **easy swing** is his own swing, the same path from the same launch, 27 ms slower over the whole swing at −3 sd; a **hard** one 20 ms quicker.

#### 6.7.4 The checks over the whole range

The oracle checks every swing of two grids (§11 case 8): the aim range (474 swings: 35 aims × attack angle ±4° × three swing times, BatSpeed ±3 sd crossed with QuickHands ±3 sd on eight aims, left-handers, effort, and the stressed jets) and the **rating range** (108 swings: the nine aims of the zone, each with BatSpeed and QuickHands at ±3 sd alone and together, effort ±3 sd, and the long corner). On every swing:

- **One surge**: before its peak, the azimuth rate never falls below its running maximum by more than 0.1% of the peak, and the sweet spot's speed by more than 2% [E] (a flat top just before contact ripples by up to 1.5%).
- **Speed rises into the ball**: the sweet spot's speed peaks at most 1.10× [E] its contact value, and no earlier than 25 ms (× T/T̄) before contact ([4]'s rate peak −10 ms, [3]'s tip peak −15 ms).
- The bat clear of the trunk and head, the hands within the arms' reach, the elevation within −45° to +65° [E: the longest swings launch with the bat up to 62°; the guard was 50° when every launch was 41°].

**In the zone every rating and effort passes, must-pass tests** (`RatingRange`): sweet spot 1.000–1.063× its contact value, azimuth rate one surge on every swing, T 0.125–0.206 s, launch −170.6° to −261.5°. **Not required, and reported**: the long corner (BatSpeed +3 sd and QuickHands −3 sd at once, λ 1.30, +4.2 sd, a swing longer than MLB's longest, note 10) dips 4% before its peak on high pitches; and outside the zone the template's own geometry for two edges: pitches at the shins on the inside edge reach 1.16× at grade 50 (1.12× 0.2 m higher; to 1.20× on long swings), and on the outside edge at the shins the hands reach past the arms' full reach (by 7.5 cm at grade 50 with 4° more uppercut, 9 cm on the slowest hands) and the speed dips 2.2%; in the zone, two low pitches swung with 4° more uppercut ripple 2.3% and 3.0% before contact (golden `aim_range.failures`). These are the elevation and hands' path for those aims and attack angles (§6.3), not the batter's travel or time: the merged path had them at grade 50 too (1.15×, the hands 5.6 cm past reach, 7.9 cm in the step-1 review).

**MLB's relations** (note 10): across ±3 sd of BatSpeed the head's travel over the last 65 ms divided by the contact speed stays within 1.5% (66.6 to 65.6 ms: MLB's 68–70 ms across speed bins, flat) and its travel from rest rises 0.054 ft per mph (MLB 0.05–0.09); within a batter the travel is the same to 1e-3 at every effort, and the time follows (0.150–0.197 s over ±3 sd).

#### 6.7.5 Timing and the decision clock

**What a real hitter does with a swing that takes longer.** MLB's tracking says he plans for it: within a batter, swings 1 mph slower are met 0.71 in deeper (note 10 §2: speed against depth r 0.29, sd 4.3 mph and 10.5 in), where a swing that took its extra 1.4% of time without starting earlier would be 3.5 in deeper (2.4 ms at about 38 m/s); and part of the 0.71 in runs the other way (a deep ball is met before the bat's peak, so slower). So at most a fifth of an easier swing's extra time shows as lateness. The model takes none of it [E]: the swing's draws (placement, timing, effort) are made at the commit, as now (`motor::execute_swing`), and its start is t_s = t_c − T for its own T. Its contact time is the planned one plus the timing draw t₀, exactly as today; an easier swing starts earlier, a harder one later.

**The decision clock** follows the batter's own swing: **t_commit = t̂_arrival − T_b − T_latency** (agent-models §3.4 with T_b his own, not a league constant). His swing to a given pitch at a given effort starts at t_c − T, which may lie up to T_latency (0.05 s) before t_c − T_b: a start inside the latency after the commit, as today's rule already allows. In the zone at ±3 sd of effort, T − T_b is at most 36 ms (x ±0.15 m) and 43 ms across the plate (tested); at the edges with an easy swing it reaches 51 ms. A start that would come before the commit is moved to the commit (`start_no_earlier_than`, as now) and the swing is late by the difference: a slow swing decided late is late, as a real one would be.

**A mid-swing re-plan** (pa.rs, every 10 ms to the cut-off) keeps the swing's T (its start is in the past) and its launch; the new aim's jet is met by letting the travel follow, λ' = λ·T/T_nominal(new aim) (the contact rate then matches exactly), with the launch held by the fade. An aim moved 5 cm moves T_nominal by up to 1.6% and adds at most 0.6% to a speed dip (10 cm: 3.0%, 1.2%; `replan.py`, and a test).

**Noise stays where it is** (invariants 1–3): `motor::execute_swing` draws δ_v, α, δ_r, t₀ from `SwingExecution` in that order and the effort from `SwingEffort`, and `apply_placement` applies them to the plan, exactly as now. v3 changes only what a planned and placed swing looks like between L and t_c. L, S and the anchors are noise-free: the load is the same on every pitch (D22, D23), and its small real variation is a look for the client. No outcome is rolled.

**Mid-swing adjustment** (pa.rs) re-plans the jet every 10 ms until the cutoff, as now, and v3 rebuilds the curves from L to the new jet each time, with t_s = t_c − T (the swing's own time, held from the commit, §6.7.5) and `start_no_earlier_than(t_commit)` exactly as v2 (so the contact time is unchanged). A re-plan also changes the path already travelled, as v2's does; nothing reads the bat's past (a brake reads the current swing at the brake instant), and the timeline encodes only the final swing, so nothing is seen.

## 8. Early, late, checked swings and the ruling

### 8.1 Early and late swings

Timing is the jet's t_c against the ball, as now: an early swing meets the ball after t_c with the bat further round (pulled, hooked), a late one before it (pushed, sliced), with the slopes of §2. A swing made late by a late decision starts later (`start_no_earlier_than`) and is the same swing shifted, as now. Each swing's own time (§6.7.5) moves its start: an easier swing or an outside pitch starts earlier, inside the latency after the commit; a start that would come before the commit is moved to it and the swing is late by the difference. The client's body timing against Δ = t_c − T_a (player-visuals §13.2) is unchanged.

### 8.2 The brake

A check swing still starts braking at t_b = (the tick when EV_swing − EV_take < −θ_check) + latency, only before t_s + 0.6·T (the swing's own time; as now). v3 brakes by slowing **the swing's own clock**: the pose at t is the path at

σ(t) = t for t ≤ t_b; σ(t) = t_b + s − s²/(2·B) with s = min(t − t_b, B), B = `BRAKE_S` = 0.040 s,

so σ advances by B/2 = 20 ms of path while slowing to a stop, the bat and hands stay on their planned path, and every velocity is the path's times σ̇ = 1 − s/B. (v2 stops each rate bump from its value at t_b, which carries the bat off its path; v3's form is the simpler and the more physical: the same move, slowed.)

### 8.3 What a checked swing does now

The brake comes at −82 ± 32 ms (baseline), and never after t_c − 10 ms (the abort point, 60 ms before contact, plus the 50 ms latency). At −82 ms v3's bat is about 160° short of its contact azimuth (the average batter at T 0.170 s; pointing away from the plate and up), and 20 ms more of path brings it to −120° to −126° (oracle, three golden aims): well back. A brake at −40 ms stops it at −41° to −45°, at −25 ms at −12° to −1°; only brakes in the last 15 ms before the cut-off (the baseline's p95 is −29 ms) carry it past 0° (at −12 ms: +16° to +31°, a swing on all three aims). A braked v3 bat almost never touches the ball and almost never passes 0°. Today 3.4% of checked swings are strikes (310 touches, 36 on the yaw), about 0.6 a game; v3 should make nearly all of them balls. No source here measures how often a real check swing is ruled a swing; this is reported and counted (D26, Q47 = a: no change to the brake or the ruling without a source).

### 8.4 rules-spec §4.5, as proposed

Replace the first sentence with: *"A batter has **swung** if the bat's horizontal direction, turned from its pose at the start of the swing in the swing's own direction, reaches the point where the bat is perpendicular to the pitcher–home line **with its barrel toward the plate** (the barrel head level with the front of the batter) [C]. The swing model reports the furthest azimuth reached, A_max (agent-models §3.6: unwrapped from the launch pose, 0° = across the plate), and the batter swung if A_max ≥ θ_swing = 0° [C, configurable]. A bat that starts wrapped behind the head passes the other perpendicular (pointing away from the plate, −180°) early in every swing; that one does not count."* The rest of §4.5 (any contact is a swing) stands.

In code: `Swing::judged_swing` becomes A(σ(t_b + B)) ≥ 0 on the unwrapped azimuth, and `max_angle` reports A_max; today's `a.y ≥ 0` test is only right for a bat that starts pointing at the catcher, and would call a wrapped bat a swing before it moves (diagnosis §2).

## 9. Determinism

- Every function is closed form in τ: polynomials, the natural cubic splines of [4]'s two curves and the exact integral of one (piecewise polynomials with constant knots and moments, §6.3), `m::sin`/`m::cos` for â, one `m::atan2`/`m::asin` for A and E of the jet. W_L and W_93 are built from the constants χ_L, χ_93 (§6.1) by closed-form vector arithmetic; nothing is solved at run time. No `mul_add`, no iteration that depends on a tolerance reached.
- No new RNG purpose. The draw order of `SwingExecution` (δ_v, α, δ_r, t₀) and `SwingEffort` is unchanged; nothing new is drawn.
- The basis matrices of §6.3 and the splines' moments are constants with a test that rederives them from their conditions and [4]'s table (the oracle exports both, `golden/swing_v3.json` `meta`).
- Same build + seed ⇒ same game, as always; v3 changes games once (build step 4), with a ledger row.

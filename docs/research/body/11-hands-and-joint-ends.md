## Part A. How a bat is held, and what the hands and forearms do around a handle

### A1. The wrist's range with the fingers closed on a handle

How I read these. Gehrmann's circles measure the **edge of the reachable region** in the two planes taken together, and the DeGroot values are the largest angle in each single direction; they are different questions and the two do not give the same total flexion (151° against 64.5 + 60 = about 125° with fingers open). Both agree on the direction: **closing the hand on a handle costs flexion first** (13–27% of the arc in Gehrmann; flexion falling from 64.5° to 20.0° at 50% grip in DeGroot's seven), costs ulnar deviation 10–11%, and leaves extension and radial deviation alone. Gehrmann's mechanism (poster discussion): flexed fingers stretch the finger extensor tendons across a flexed wrist; the thumb tendons across an ulnar-deviated wrist. Neither study held a bat or swung; their handle weighed under 0.3 N and nothing pulled on it.

**The range is not a box.** The spec's table has the wrist at 58° of cock (22° radial, 36° ulnar; Boone & Azen through Norkin & White) and 151° of flexion–extension (76° and 75°). Gehrmann's circle gives **80° of radial–ulnar travel** with free fingers, 22° more than the box, because the extremes of one direction are reached with the other direction off neutral. Three things bear on the shape:

- **The joint's own stiffness is oblique.** Crisco et al., J Bone Joint Surg Am 2011;93:1 (PMC3016043): six fresh-frozen cadaver wrists, unconstrained moments of **±2 N·m** applied in 24 directions; the range-of-motion envelope was an ellipse tilted **26.6 ± 4.4°** from pure flexion–extension; the largest range was **111.5 ± 10.2°**, toward ulnar flexion about 30° from pure flexion; stiffness was largest toward radial flexion (0.4 N·m/°) and smallest toward ulnar flexion (0.15 N·m/°). Cadavers, passive, inside ±2 N·m: it says where the wrist is loose, not how it resists past its end. **AB** (the paper's abstract and the open text's summary).
- **The "dart thrower's" plane.** The 2007 IFSSH committee report (Moritomo et al., J Hand Surg Am 32:1447; **FT**, excerpt) defines it as the wrist's functional oblique motion from radial extension to ulnar flexion, in a plane about **30–45° from the sagittal plane**; Palmer et al. 1985 found hammering to run from about 40° of extension with 20° of radial deviation to about 0° with 20° of ulnar deviation, **not through neutral**. A mallet-swinging study (Mitsukane et al. 2025, PMC12147615; 42 healthy adults, optical capture; **TAB**) found the plane about **30° to the sagittal plane**, offset 25.8–31.8° toward extension, with a range of **50.8 ± 13.1°** (dominant hand). Goniometer arcs from radial extension to ulnar flexion: men 126° (range 83–166°) dominant, 114° (62–148°) non-dominant, 200 volunteers aged 18–25, 63% women (Leventhal 2021, J Hand Surg; **AB**).
- **Ranges used in daily tasks are smaller than active ranges**: 60° extension, 54° flexion, 40° ulnar, 17° radial deviation covered a battery of tasks in 40 adults (Ryu et al., J Hand Surg Am 1991;16:409; **AB**). Gracia-Ibáñez et al. (J Biomech 2020, 109975; **EXC**) fit ellipses to the coupled angles used in 32 tasks by 18 adults and note that free circumduction reaches a high-flexion, ulnar-deviation corner that those tasks never use, and that gripping a cylinder shrinks it (citing Gehrmann).

Whether a swing uses the DTM plane is not stated by any baseball source I found; the golf data in A5 are in the anatomical axes, not in an oblique plane.

### A3. Wrist strength with a gripped handle

Note 9 has Holzbaur (5 men, isometric flexion 24–26, extension 13–14 N·m), Peleg (20 men, ulnar deviation 26.5, radial 14.9 N·m at 90°/s) and Kentel's tennis player. Added here:

- Ten men, 25.5 y (range 22–31), a force-sensor handle, forearm clamped, isometric 5 s, twice: **flexion 8.0 ± 3.0 N·m (forearm neutral), 8.3 ± 3.1 (pronated), 11.9 ± 2.9 (supinated); extension 4.6 ± 1.0, 6.5 ± 1.4, 5.5 ± 1.2** (Biomed Eng Online 2015;14:115, PMC4676844; **EXC**, first author not captured). Forearm position moves wrist strength by up to 50%; note 9's 24–26 N·m flexion came from a different method and the two sets disagree by a factor of two to three. I cannot say which is nearer to a hand on a bat.
- Grip strength depends on the wrist's angle: highest at slight extension (median **16.5°** over 9 subjects, range 0.5–50°; Ambike et al., PMC3766344, quasi-static, **EXC**); precision-grip force peaked at 30° of extension in 15 adults and fell 10–20% at 20–50° of flexion (Popp et al. 2023, J Neurophysiol; **EXC**); 37 tennis players' grip force at maximal wrist positions: neutral 80.2 ± 22.1 kg, extension 57.0 ± 18.4, **flexion 34.0 ± 9.5**, radial 56.3 ± 19.4, ulnar 56.6 ± 17.6 (ISBS conference record; **EXC**). A wrist forced into flexion keeps about 40% of its neutral-wrist grip.
- Athletes' wrist torques at speed: nothing beyond Laudner's untabulated 90 and 180°/s values (above).

### A4. Whether the bat moves in the hands

What the instrumented bats measured, and what they cannot say:

- **Koike, Kimura, Kawamura, Fujii, Takahashi & Ae, ISB 2003 long abstract (FT).** Five varsity players (68–79 kg, 1.69–1.82 m), tee batting at hip height, bat cut at the handle and rejoined by an aluminium bar with strain gauges that read the torsional moment about the grip axis between the hands, the bending moments and the axial forces; 500 Hz, Vicon at 120 Hz. The bottom (knob-side) hand does most of the accelerating; the top hand "modifies" and, just before impact, pushes the other way (its force along the bat turns negative). The two hands' forces form couples. The moment of the bottom hand about the bat's z-axis peaks about 140% through (of the phase times, where 100% is the downswing's start and 300% impact) and falls rapidly toward impact. **The plots carry the numbers; the abstract gives none, so I have no N·m**. The same bat in Koike's later work: Ae, Burke, Kawamura & Koike 2022 (J Appl Biomech 38:103; 23 collegiate players, tee, 28 strain gauges; **AB**) and Ae & Koike 2011 (ISBS 29; **AB**).
- **Miyanishi, ISBS 2009 (FT).** Eight varsity batters (19 ± 1 y, 1.71 m, 71 kg), 120 km/h machine, two high-speed cameras at 250 Hz, landmarks smoothed with quintic splines at **4–20 Hz** cut-offs; the grip's contributions to the bat head's 36.5 ± 2.9 m/s: the **force along the bat 54 ± 4%**, the **torque normal to the bat's axis 49 ± 13%** (the higher-speed half 52 ± 14, the lower 47 ± 13: "the snap of the grip" may differ), others at most 4%, the normal force's share −12 ± 9%. The torque here is the couple the two hands apply across the handle, which a rigid welded handle supplies without any grip slip; it does not say whether the handle turns in the palm.
- **Crisco, Osvalds & Rainbow, J Appl Biomech 2018;34:386 (AB).** 22 male players aged 13–18, 306 swings, three youth bats; inverse dynamics about the batter's hands from the bat's known motion: the force is mostly **along the bat away from the knob**, and **the moment on the bat is small until just before impact, then rises quickly**.
- **Finger pressures, Takahashi et al., Kanagawa Tai-iku Kenkyu 2011;44:11 (Japanese; EXC).** Six experienced players, tee, pressure sensors on gloves at 100 Hz: at impact the knob-side hand's ring finger carried the largest pressure; the barrel-side hand "hardly any" except one site; the grip diameter judged easiest to hold gave a lower impact pressure and less bat vibration. The authors read this as squeezing hard in the swing and not at impact.
- **Pressure over the palm.** Flynn et al., Orthop J Sports Med 2021;9(12) (PMC8671670; **TAB**): 14 NCAA D-I players, soft toss, 12 cameras at 200 Hz, **markers on the lower hand's metacarpal only**; peak pressure over the hook of the hamate, bare hand: 1.36 ± 0.73 kg/cm² in a full swing with all fingers on the bat, 1.68 ± 1.17 with one finger off, 1.18 ± 0.96 choked up, **3.72 ± 2.64 in a check swing with one finger off**. The same paper's wrist numbers are in A5.

**No source I found measured the handle turning, pivoting or sliding in either hand** (no motion of the bat relative to the hand's markers is reported by any of the above). The golf instrumented grips (A6) are the nearest. I list this under Not found.

These are for checking a built grip afterwards, not targets (PLAN D37). Every source's processing is stated.

**OpenBiomechanics Project, hitting (Driveline), computed by me from the open `hitting_joint_angles.zip` (release `dataset-v1`): 677 swings, 650 with the foot-plant and contact times.** Licence: cross-check only ([note 7](7-datasets-and-licences.md)). **mine**. Hitters: mostly college, a few professionals. Foot plant is the lead foot at 100% of body weight (`fp_100`); contact is OBP's contact event. Reading. **Lead wrist:** extended about 19° at foot plant, flexing to about 8° of flexion at contact (the mean traverse is about 27° of flexion), tilting from radial to **16° ulnar** (the "cock" releasing); the largest single-swing excursions in 0.5 s run to 46° (flexion/extension) and 33° (radial/ulnar) as medians. **Rear wrist:** **strongly extended through the swing, 37–77° on average, 47° at contact**: it hardly flexes (the range 33° is mostly extension coming back from about 77° early), and its radial–ulnar travel is smaller (about 20°). The forearm turns: rear forearm third angle changes **about 50°** between foot plant and contact (median), the lead about 20°. Single-swing maxima in the file reach 220° (rear wrist) and 188° (rear elbow third angle), which are wraps or tracking gaps; I report percentiles for that reason. Sign and zero conventions are the source's own and I have not checked them against a goniometer: the rear wrist's +47° extension at contact and the +77° early peak sit near the clinical extension limit of 70–76°, which is worth testing against the same source's marker set before trusting the early peak.

Golf has the wrist data baseball lacks and it agrees in direction with the baseball lead wrist (flexion and ulnar travel together, the trailing hand extended behind). It differs in the loads: a driver's 45 m/s head and a 0.3–0.4 kg club against a bat's 29–34 m/s sweet spot and 0.9 kg. Choi & Park's six-axis grip had the head-side hand (the trail hand) applying three times the grip-end hand's torque; Koike's baseball bat had the knob-side hand doing most of the accelerating and the top hand "modifying". Both are small samples; I read them as the two hands having different jobs, not as one hand's number carrying over to the other sport.

### A7. How stiff and how lossy is a grip, and how much torque can a hand hold about the handle

**Rotational stiffness and damping of a hand around a handle's own axis.**

- Schröder, Lindenmann & Matthiesen, Vibration 2023;6:23 (MDPI; **FT** excerpts): 13 adults, a shaker driving a cylindrical handle (taped for friction) around its axis, 10–100 Hz, grip forces 5.5–24 N and push forces 15–50 N: the response is **a spring and a damper**, with a phase near −π/4 from 16 to 80 Hz; the magnitude rises with grip force and is not changed by push force or posture. The magnitudes in N·m·s/rad are in the paper's figures; the excerpt has none. Spengler, Saurbier & Matthiesen (KIT data set, 2025; **AB**): 10 subjects, grip 70–135 N, push 25–65 N, 10–100 Hz, the impedance in N·m·s/rad and the apparent moment of inertia in kg·m² (the numbers are in a MATLAB file I did not open). What this cannot support: the motion is a vibration at 6 m/s², small and fast, so it measures skin and tissue; it says nothing about a handle rolling through tens of degrees in a palm.
- A hand–arm model's torsional spring–damper: an impact-wrench paper (ter Braack & Margolis, Machines 2026;14:213, **EXC**) tunes its hand's three rotational attachment frequencies to 1–25 Hz, 30–42 Hz and 60–132 Hz by axis, a design choice and not a measurement.
- Stiffness of the **wrist** inside its range: [note 9](9-arm-limits.md) has Formica (0.6–1.9 N·m/rad) and Crisco's ellipse above (0.15–0.4 N·m/°, which is 8.6–23 N·m/rad). The two differ by a factor of 10–15; Crisco's is the cadaver wrist at ±2 N·m, Formica's the living one.

| Kong & Lowe, Int J Ind Ergon 2005;35:1073 (CDC copy) | 25–50 mm handles, a 16-sensor force glove, LIDO work simulator, clockwise (screwdriver-like) maximum voluntary torque | mean **4.10 N·m (handle horizontal), 3.14 (vertical)**; men 4.0, women 3.24; the largest with the 45–50 mm handles, the smallest with 25 mm; total finger force **477 N at 25 mm falling to 343 N at 50 mm** | **EXC** |
| a vertical-handle follow-up (CDC 196077) | the same set-up, 25–50 mm | **4.38 N·m (25 mm), 7.89 (45 mm), 7.80 (50 mm)** | **EXC** |
| Seo, Armstrong, Ashton-Miller & Chaffin, J Biomech 2007; Seo et al., Ergonomics 2011;54:961 (PMC8856563) | maximum isometric torque about a vertical cylinder's long axis, pressure sensors; circular handles of 25.4 and 38.1 mm radius, aluminium and rubber | ratio of torque to total normal force, measured: aluminium 17.6 mm (medium), 26.9 (large); rubber 27.9 (medium), 39.2 (large) (friction coefficients 0.85 aluminium, 1.43 rubber from Seo 2009); twisting inward (toward the fingertips) gave 45% more torque in Seo's thesis | **EXC** |

The reading: **a bare hand holds on the order of 3–8 N·m about a handle's own axis before it slips**, depending on diameter, grip force and surface, and the best case (45–50 mm, forearm free) is about 8 N·m; the slip limit is friction × squeeze × the handle's radius, T = μ·G·r, so a harder squeeze raises it in proportion (for a 33 mm bat handle, a 500 N squeeze and the 0.85–1.43 of Seo's two surfaces, the formula would give 7–12 N·m with a uniform pressure; this is arithmetic on the cited μ and not a measurement). None of these tasks was a swing: the load arrives in milliseconds with the hand's tissue already deformed.

**Torque across the handle (about its two perpendicular axes).** The bat's bending moment between the hands is supplied by the couple of the two hands' forces, so it is a force problem and not a grip-slip problem; Koike's and Miyanishi's couples (A4) are the evidence and the numbers sit in plots. Crisco 2018 (youth bats): moments small until just before impact. **No number for the torque a hand can hold across a handle was found.**

## Part B. A joint at and past the end of its range

PLAN D39 (owner, Q61 = b): the end of a joint's range takes the motion's energy and gives none back, and a heavy load on it counts against a practice search; both sizes are estimates [E] unless a measurement is found. What follows is what was measured. **The short answer is that nobody loaded a living joint past its end and recorded the moment**, so the passive curves below stop at or just inside the end, the energy figures are for stretches inside the range, and the failure loads are cadavers or net inverse-dynamics torques. A search worker read most of these sources under the same rules ([note 10](10-smoothed-sources.md)'s style) and I took its file as the source for B; where I could only reach an abstract it says so.

### B1. Passive moment against angle near the end of range

**Lower limb, sagittal (hip, knee, ankle).**

- **Riener & Edrich 1999 (J Biomech 32:539; 10 healthy adults lying down, a limb segment moved slowly through a hand-held load cell, the gravity moment subtracted, other joints held; the paper's abstract: AB).** The primary text could not be opened. Its equations reached me **2nd**: Héra & Zalavári 2014 quote the hip, in the original signs, as `M = exp(1.4655 − 0.0034·θK − 0.0750·θH) − exp(1.3403 − 0.0226·θK + 0.0305·θH) + 8.072` N·m (angles in degrees, θH hip, θK knee), and say that the model and the data differ "at the end of the range of motion". Gasparutto, Jacquelin & Dumas 2018 (Proc IMechE H 232:768, TAB of their Table 1) reprint hip, knee and ankle with their axes re-signed to ISB: constants α/β (hip 1.4655/1.3403; knee −3.9710/1.8000; ankle −7.9763/2.1016), additive constants 8.072, 4.820 and 1.792 N·m, and a knee term `−exp(2.2200 + 0.150·θK)`. **The two disagree on the sign of the knee-angle terms and I could not settle which is right for which axis**, and I did not evaluate either. Do not use the equations without the primary paper.
- **Silder, Whittington, Heiderscheit & Thelen, J Biomech 2007 (PMC2020832; FT).** 20 healthy adults (9 men, 26.1 ± 4.1 y, 79.2 kg), side-lying, a therapist moved the limb through its sagittal range slowly on carts through load cells; exponentials `M = exp(β(θ − α))` fitted per muscle group. Offset angles α (where the moment reaches 1 N·m): hip flexors 19.5 ± 10.0°, hip extensors 27.3 ± 18.0°, hamstrings 30.8 ± 14.9°, knee extensors 101.9 ± 11.9°, plantarflexors −3.4 ± 5.0° (gains and the unit of β are not clear in my extract). Fit error 2.5 N·m hip, 1.4 knee, 0.7 ankle. The authors: the end-range moments vary more between people than mid-range, "a small deviation in joint angle can cause a large change in the estimated moment", and the data show "slight" hysteresis that the model does not carry, so fitting both directions "likely results in … an over-estimation of the passive energy returned". The end-range N·m are in a figure I did not read.
- Other published passive models, 2nd through Gasparutto: Amankwah (5 adults, double exponentials for hip, knee, ankle in several axes); a linear "ligament" model with stiffness 0.7 N·m/° at the hip beyond 20°, 0.27 at the knee beyond −25°, 0.21 at the ankle beyond −15° (Al Nazer); Audu & Davy's exponentials (hip c = 2.6, d = 8.7 N·m, a = 5.8, b = 1.3 per rad). Gasparutto judged the linear and Audu–Davy models to "overestimate" the ligaments' action. All 2nd; the printed equations are partly garbled.

**The upper limb and the trunk.**

- **The shoulder and elbow beyond their range: Engin & Chen 1986/1987** (J Biomech Eng 108:222, 109:318; Peindl & Engin 1987). Ten male subjects, an applicator pushed the arm past the voluntary range to a "maximal forced sinus" and the resisting moment was fitted by spherical-harmonic coefficients and shown as contour maps. **AB only; no moment in N·m was reachable.** The 1979 paper's abstract says the tolerable passive torques about the long bones of the leg are more than twice those of the arm (three subjects). This is the one set that went past the end of range and I could not read it.
- **Wrist: Delp, Grierson & Buchanan 1996 (J Biomech; AB and the PDF's text).** 10 healthy men, seated, flexion–extension moments every 20° over the middle 80° and every 10° to the end: the passive moment is near zero over the central 150°; **0.5 N·m at 90° of flexion, 1.2 N·m at 90° of extension; 0.9 N·m at full ulnar deviation**. Four of the ten could not reach 90° of flexion because of discomfort, so the mean at 90° is probably low (the authors say so). Crisco's cadaver wrist (A1) had 0.15–0.4 N·m/° at ≤ ±2 N·m. Different subjects (living, cadaver) and ranges; not comparable.
- **Trunk, in vivo.** McGill, Seguin & Bennett 1994 (Spine; AB: 22 men and 15 women, torso floating on a jig, pulled by a transducer, muscles silent; "torsos are stiffer in lateral bending and store more elastic energy"). Their curves reach me through the Watt, Callaway & Williams 2022 meta-analysis (Med Biol Eng Comput 60:2133, PMC9293810, FT of its tables; **2nd for McGill**): stiffness in N·m per degree at 2°, 4° … for **flexion 0.29, 0.36, 0.45, 0.56, 0.69, 0.86, 1.08, 1.34 at 2–16°; extension 0.17 … 0.86 at 2–22°; clockwise twist 0.13 … 0.64 at 2–24°**. Pooled over 13 studies and 354 adults under 60, in the four quarters of the range: flexion 0.34, 0.65, 1.28, **2.36**; lateral bend 0.32, 0.83, 2.03, **5.03**; twist 0.21, 0.34, 0.71, 1.33 N·m/° (the last quarter of twist from McGill alone). Gombatto's prone lateral-bend data (19 adults) have the last quarter about 20 times the first. Kosmopoulos et al., Open Spine J 2012 (18 adults, twisting moments of 3.7 to 22 N·m): **20.3 ± 5.8 N·m/rad (right) and 17.4 ± 4.9 (left)**, which is 0.35 and 0.30 N·m/°, over **3.7–22 N·m only**. Drake & Callaghan 2007 (10 men; AB): twist stiffness 81% of neutral in full flexion and 125% in extension; the twist range 14% more in flexion, 24% less in extension. Lee & McGill (24 men, in Watt's table): flexion reached 19.7, 22.1, 25.9, 31.8, 34.2, **35.6°** at 50, 65, 80, 90, 95, 100% of the largest applied moment (extension 12.4 … 25.6°), a **maximum moment not stated in what I read**. The authors of Watt say the latter stages of twist need more study. Cadaver lumbar segments, per segment, 14 men (mean age 29): stiffness 5.5 N·m/° in flexion at 68.6 N·m, 7.6 extension, 4.4 lateral bend at 58.8, 10.9 torsion at 68.6 N·m (EXC, OCR errors) — a different quantity from the whole-torso numbers above (4–11 N·m/° against 0.3–1.3 in the first three quarters) because cadavers, a single segment and loads of 60–95 N·m.
- **The ankle and the hamstrings, to where an examiner stops.** Fukaya et al., Sports 2020;8:140 (FT excerpt): 65 young adults, Biodex, knee straight, passive dorsiflexion at 5°/s to pain or discomfort, **26.6 ± 9.5° and 37.4 ± 14.4 N·m** at the end (37.6 ± 14.2 after a 120 s stretch). Obst et al., J Sports Sci 2023 (TAB): 22 adults, a window ending at 20° of dorsiflexion (or each person's tolerance), **15.8 ± 5.5 N·m** at 5°/s; stiffness 0.15 then 0.54–0.58 N·m/°. The factor of 2.4 between the two is the end criterion (discomfort against a fixed window), not the subjects. Takeuchi 2021 (12 men) and Palmer 2015 (13 adults) took hamstrings to discomfort; the N·m are in figures. Magnusson et al. 2000 (AB): the angle tolerated was 1.30 rad in 7 flexible and 0.84 rad in 6 inflexible people at tensions of 81.8 ± 12.5 and 29.3 ± 4.1 N/cm² — the same tissue tolerated very different loads, so the end of range is a tolerance and not a tissue limit.

**Where a model's joint-end stiffness has been taken from.** OpenSim's `CoordinateLimitForce` (docs; **FT**): an upper and a lower stiffness, an upper and a lower limit, a damping coefficient, and a transition width over which the stiffness rises from 0 to its constant value ("C2 continuous"); rotational stiffness in N·m/°, damping in N·m/(°/s). Defaults from its tests and tutorial are tool settings (a tutorial's designed ankle spring: 10 N·m/° from 5°, 0.01 damping, 2° transition; a unit test's 200/1000 and 0.01 on a translational coordinate), not anatomy. **Saul et al. 2015 (PMC4282829; TAB, Table 2)** give the upper limb's: shoulder elevation upper limit 150°, stiffness 100 N·m/°, transition 543°; elbow flexion 85°, 0.30 N·m/°, transition 140°; forearm 60°, 50 N·m/°, 430°; wrist flexion 20°, 52.6 N·m/°, 39°; wrist deviation 60°, 200 N·m/°, 93°, with damping 0.1 N·m·s/rad. Their own sentence: the parameters are "only a mathematical fit, and do not result in physiologically meaningful coefficients" (a fit to the elastic torque curves of Rankin 2010 and Velisar & Murray 2005), and the transitions of 39–545° mean the constant stiffness is **never reached inside any real range**. Experimental data for damping "is needed" (authors). **I found no published limit stiffness for Rajagopal 2016 or Holzbaur 2005** (Rajagopal uses passive Hill fibres and says that at extreme angles its model gives *higher* passive torque than experiment). Roth et al. 2011 (4 subjects, mid-range small motions; EXC) give lumped k of 1.9–8.5 N·m/rad (wrist), 1.7–3 (elbow), 19–60 (shoulder).

Hysteresis here is (work put in − work returned) / work put in; the share returned is 100% minus it.

So **in vivo, what comes back is most of it**: 78–87% for a tendon or a whole ankle cycled slowly inside its range, with the loss growing with stretch speed (13.4 → 16.8% from 5 to 20°/s). That is a different quantity from D39's end of range, for three reasons the sources state or imply: all were inside the range or at a tolerance and none at a load that hurts; none went above 180°/s (the fastest, a velocity-torque slope), whereas a swing's joints run at 5–20 rad/s; and the tendon figures are for a contraction. **A single study at a joint's end that put in energy and measured what came back was not found, for any joint**, and nothing was found for the shoulder, hip, wrist or trunk at all (McGill's torso is "capable of storing greater elastic energy" but he gave no percentage in the abstract). The one thing that can be said about the size of the loss is its range across studies: 3.8–37%, 3.8% in a pulsed-shortwave paper's before-heating figure for an ankle (EXC; n not read) and 15.4% after, so the same measure moved four times with the tissue's state.

### B3. What load a joint's end tolerates

**The elbow's inner ligament (ulnar collateral, UCL), the best known.**

- **Cadavers, ultimate valgus moment.** Ahmad, Lee & ElAttrache, Am J Sports Med 2003;31 (AB): 10 matched pairs, **34.0 ± 6.9 N·m**, stiffness 42.8 N/mm (the elbow at 70° of flexion, 10 men, age 43, per Van Trigt's table, 2nd). McGraw 2013: 35.0 ± 14.0 N·m (10 specimens, 52 y, 30°); Hechtman 1998: 22.7 ± 9.0 (31 specimens); Dillman 1991: 32.9 ± 5.4 (11); Paletta (UBC wiki's account): 17.1–18.8 N·m (cyclic to failure; table and text disagree); a Smith & Bernholt 2020 sentence: 17.1–22.7 N·m "in elderly cadavers", 34 in the younger series. Van Trigt 2021 (FT): "approximately 30 N·m" in all. Static, slow pulls, mostly older specimens (2nd).
- **Pitchers, net varus torque by inverse dynamics (not the ligament's own load):** **64 ± 12 N·m** at 66% of the time from foot contact to release, the elbow bent 95 ± 10° (Fleisig, Andrews, Dillman & Escamilla, Am J Sports Med 1995;23:233: 26 healthy pitchers, 22 ± 2.3 y, 38.3 m/s; **AB** and the dissertation text, EXC); **120 N·m** in 7 college and minor-league pitchers (Werner et al., JOSPT 1993;17:274: AB, EXC), whose authors wrote that "previous cadaveric research showed that the UCL by itself cannot withstand a valgus load of this magnitude"; Feltner & Dapena about 100 (2nd). Reviews quote a spread of 45–120 N·m for adults, 18–27 for youth, a mean 60 N·m over 82,000 throws by 81 professionals with individual means 41–94 (2nd). The UCL is said to carry 54% of the varus torque at 90° of flexion (Morrey, 2nd), about 35 N·m of 64. The rest of the torque is taken by the muscles (the flexor–pronator group) and the bone; Van Trigt reads the mismatch as other structures sharing the load.
- Not found: any moment for elbow hyperextension (the end of extension) or for the end of flexion.

**Other joints.**

- **Knee, cadavers.** Meyer & Haut, J Biomech 2008;41:3377 (AB): 7 pairs, repetitive loading to injury: the ACL failed at **33 ± 13 N·m of internal tibial torque** (58 ± 19° of internal rotation) or 5.4 ± 2 kN of compression; Oh et al. 2011 call those rotations "quite large" because muscle forces were absent. Meyer, Villwock & Haut 2009 (AB): 4 pairs of knees, mean age 40, valgus bending to injury at **107 ± 64 N·m** (the MCL in 7 knees, the ACL in 3). Non-destructive: Fukuda 2003 (10 knees, 0–10 N·m of valgus); Griffith, LaPrade 2009 (24 knees, 10 N·m valgus and 5 N·m rotation). ACL 640–2,100 N and MCL about 2,300 N as forces (2nd).
- **Shoulder, cadavers.** Stefko et al. 1997 (AB): the inferior glenohumeral ligament and capsule failed at **712.9 ± 238.2 N** (7.2 ± 2.3% strain) in 13 shoulders at 90° of abduction and the apprehension position of external rotation; a force, not a torque. Kuhn 2005 (AB; 15 shoulders): the order of restraint, no N·m. In pitchers: 67 ± 11 N·m internal-rotation torque and 97 N·m horizontal-abduction torque, net (Fleisig 1995).
- **Wrist.** Hockey-wrist study (PMC6281460, FT): 7 cadaver arms, mean age 69.5, the dorsal ulnotriquetral ligament loaded in radial deviation at 0.5°/s yielded at **12.93 ± 5.20 N·m**, 0.35 N·m/°, 57.7 ± 20.6° of deformation. Distal radius fracture forces 1,600–3,400 N (2nd).
- Not found: the ultimate external-rotation torque of a shoulder, the failure moment of a hip, a wrist's flexion–extension failure moment, knee or elbow hyperextension.

**What an examiner's push amounts to at a range's end:** the ankle at discomfort 37.4 ± 14.4 N·m (Fukaya) or 15.8 N·m at a fixed window (Obst); 22 N·m on the trunk's twist (Kosmopoulos, not the end); the wrist 0.5–1.2 N·m (Delp); cadaver knees 5 N·m of rotation and 10 N·m of valgus in non-destructive tests. Engin & Chen's applicator, the one that pressed to the "forced sinus", has no figure that I could read. Examiners in Wilk's pitchers pressed on the olecranon until the scapula moved and gave no force. The spec's 50 N·m ("about what an examiner's push at the end of a large joint's range amounts to") has no measurement that I found behind it: the closest are the ankle's 37 N·m, the UCL's 35 N·m at failure (cadaver) and 64 N·m (net) in a pitch.

### B4. Leftovers from the spec's table

**Wilk 2012's zero.** Wilk, Macrina & Arrigo, Clin Orthop Relat Res 2012 (PMC3348299; FT): 369 professional pitchers of one organisation, spring training 2005–2010, supine and passive, the scapula's lateral border held by the examiner, pressure on the olecranon, the end when the scapula moved and a "capsular-like" end-feel came: **42 ± 8° dominant, 44 ± 8° non-dominant**, p = 0.001. The goniometer's axis is over the olecranon, one arm perpendicular to the ground, the other along the ulna. **The paper never states the zero or the starting arm position.** Its only nearby wording is that the rotation test position is "90° of abduction and in the scapular plane (10° of horizontal adduction from the coronal plane)", which counts the angle from the coronal plane (the arm out to the side) but belongs to the rotation test, not to this one. Other papers using the same supine, scapula-stabilised test put the zero in different places: Laudner et al. (J Athl Train, PMC1748419; 23 professional pitchers; "0° of horizontal adduction (perpendicular plane to the table)", i.e. the coronal plane by my reading); Lim 2015 (the arm flexed 90°, pointing up when supine); Hall 2020 (an angle to the horizontal at 90° elevation). Values with those methods: about 83° pooled in non-overhead controls (n = 61, the NSCA review's quotation, EXC), 154.1 ± 7.1° in 24 swimmers (Cejudo 2019), 135° as the clinical reference. **They are not one quantity and cannot be put on an axis from what is printed.** My own arithmetic, in no source: 135° − 90° = 45°, close to Wilk's 42–44°, which would fit a zero at the arm pointing straight forward; nothing in the text says it. So the spec's reading (132° from the arm out to the side) is not supported or ruled out; the zero stays unresolved.

**How far an arm can be drawn back (horizontal abduction/extension).** Cejudo et al., Front Psychol 2019 (PMC6414446; TAB): 24 competitive young swimmers (15.6 ± 2.2 y; 48 shoulders), passive, inclinometer, the end at compensation or a strong but tolerable stretch: **horizontal abduction 39.8 ± 5.5°** (41.5 ± 5.5 pain-free, 36.6 ± 4.1 in those who developed pain); shoulder extension 91.3 ± 9.5°. Reference values the paper cites: 30–45° in adults (Gerhardt 1994; Clarkson 2003), 44° in 28 college swimmers (2nd), 0° as the clinical cut-off, and Norkin & White's 45° (2nd, through Physiopedia, from the arm abducted 90°). Fleisig et al., JSES Int 2022 (PMC9937824; FT): active motion by markerless capture in 6,635 people aged 10 to 70+; females 5–10° more than males at 10–59 and the men's horizontal abduction does not change with age; **the numeric means are in an appendix I could not download**. The zero of Cejudo's "horizontal" is not stated either. Nothing for the pitching arm.

**The trunk's ranges.** None of the AAOS tables, Dvorak, Tousignant or the Fitzgerald table could be reached as numbers. What was: lumbar only, Troke et al. 2001/2005 (405 asymptomatic people, 209 men, 16–90 y, CA6000 analyser, standing; AB): flexion 73° falling to 40° with age, extension 29° to 6–7°, lateral flexion 28° to 14° each side (total 58° to 30°), axial rotation about 7° each way; Uluçam & Cigali 2009 (100 students, 18–22 y, ultrasound; EXC): flexion 61.6 ± 8.1°, extension 19.4 ± 8.3°, total lateral flexion 51.3 ± 7.5° (about 26° a side) in the men, the pelvis supplying 46.6% of the lumbosacral flexion; thoracolumbar goniometry in 160 Korean adults (AB): flexion 53.1 ± 12.9°, extension 39.9 ± 12.2°, lateral flexion 46.2 ± 8.9° (per side or total not stated), rotation 27.6 ± 7.3° and 29.5 ± 8.2°; Fitzgerald 1983 (172 subjects, almost all men; AB): the abstract has no numbers, and a slide deck quoting Norkin (2nd) puts thoracolumbar lateral flexion at "0–35°" and the Fitzgerald mean at 37.6° at 20–29 y falling to 18.0° at 70–79 y. These disagree (lumbar twist 7° a side against thoracolumbar 28–30°) because the thoracic spine gives most of the twist, and because of the method (tape, goniometer, ultrasound), the region and the age; none of the papers reconciles them. [Note 12](12-trunk-bending-and-feet.md) §2 has where the bending is.

## What this gives the design

1. The wrist's range shrinks with a closed hand (flexion first, 13–27%), and its edge is an oblique ellipse (80° of radial–ulnar travel at the edge of a circle against a 58° box); measured lead and rear wrist angles through a swing exist for checking (A5), with the lead wrist ending near 8–19° flexed and 16° ulnar and the rear wrist staying extended near 47°.
2. The forearm's turn is 10–13 N·m isometric in active men (Köykkä's 20), 13.5–16.2 in another 24, 17–18 in one tennis player: the three differ by the lever and the posture, and no strength at speed was found.
3. Nobody measured a bat turning in a hand, nor the torque a hand can hold across a handle. A bare hand holds 3–8 N·m about a handle's own axis (to 12 N·m is arithmetic on μ·G·r, not a measurement); the hand's rotational impedance is a spring and a damper measured only as small vibrations.
4. For a joint's end there are slopes **inside** the range (the trunk's 0.3–1.3 N·m/° in the first three quarters, 2.4 at the last quarter of flexion and 5 at the last quarter of lateral bend; the wrist 0.5–1.2 N·m at ±90°), and the only models that carry an end are fits whose own authors call them non-physical. No measured stiffness past the end.
5. What comes back from a stretch: 78–87% for a tendon or an ankle cycled slowly in the range; falling with speed; **no measurement at a joint's end, at a swing's speeds, or for any joint but the ankle, knee and calf**. D39's "gives none back" is not contradicted or supported by anything measured at the end.
6. Loads: the elbow's ligament fails at about 34 N·m in cadavers, the knee's ACL at 33 N·m of twist, a cadaver knee at 107 N·m of valgus, a wrist ligament at 13 N·m, the ankle was held to 37 N·m at discomfort by a dynamometer; pitchers' net elbow varus torque is 64–120 N·m. The spec's 50 N·m for "a heavy load" sits between the cadaver failure loads and the pitchers' net torques, and no study sets it.

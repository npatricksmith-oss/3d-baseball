## Part A. How a bat is held, and what the hands and forearms do around a handle

### A1. The wrist's range with the fingers closed on a handle

How I read these. Gehrmann's circles measure the **edge of the reachable region** in the two planes taken together, and the DeGroot values are the largest angle in each single direction; they are different questions and the two do not give the same total flexion (151° against 64.5 + 60 = about 125° with fingers open). Both agree on the direction: **closing the hand on a handle costs flexion first** (13–27% of the arc in Gehrmann; flexion falling from 64.5° to 20.0° at 50% grip in DeGroot's seven), costs ulnar deviation 10–11%, and leaves extension and radial deviation alone. Gehrmann's mechanism (poster discussion): flexed fingers stretch the finger extensor tendons across a flexed wrist; the thumb tendons across an ulnar-deviated wrist. Neither study held a bat or swung; their handle weighed under 0.3 N and nothing pulled on it.

**The range is not a box.** The spec's table has the wrist at 58° of cock (22° radial, 36° ulnar; Boone & Azen through Norkin & White) and 151° of flexion–extension (76° and 75°). Gehrmann's circle gives **80° of radial–ulnar travel** with free fingers, 22° more than the box, because the extremes of one direction are reached with the other direction off neutral. Three things bear on the shape:

- **The joint's own stiffness is oblique.** Crisco et al., J Bone Joint Surg Am 2011;93:1 (PMC3016043): six fresh-frozen cadaver wrists, unconstrained moments of **±2 N·m** applied in 24 directions; the range-of-motion envelope was an ellipse tilted **26.6 ± 4.4°** from pure flexion–extension; the largest range was **111.5 ± 10.2°**, toward ulnar flexion about 30° from pure flexion; stiffness was largest toward radial flexion (0.4 N·m/°) and smallest toward ulnar flexion (0.15 N·m/°). Cadavers, passive, inside ±2 N·m: it says where the wrist is loose, not how it resists past its end. **AB** (the paper's abstract and the open text's summary).
- **The "dart thrower's" plane.** The 2007 IFSSH committee report (Moritomo et al., J Hand Surg Am 32:1447; **FT**, excerpt) defines it as the wrist's functional oblique motion from radial extension to ulnar flexion, in a plane about **30–45° from the sagittal plane**; Palmer et al. 1985 found hammering to run from about 40° of extension with 20° of radial deviation to about 0° with 20° of ulnar deviation, **not through neutral**. A mallet-swinging study (Mitsukane et al. 2025, PMC12147615; 42 healthy adults, optical capture; **TAB**) found the plane about **30° to the sagittal plane**, offset 25.8–31.8° toward extension, with a range of **50.8 ± 13.1°** (dominant hand). Goniometer arcs from radial extension to ulnar flexion: men 126° (range 83–166°) dominant, 114° (62–148°) non-dominant, 200 volunteers aged 18–25, 63% women (Leventhal 2021, J Hand Surg; **AB**).
- **Ranges used in daily tasks are smaller than active ranges**: 60° extension, 54° flexion, 40° ulnar, 17° radial deviation covered a battery of tasks in 40 adults (Ryu et al., J Hand Surg Am 1991;16:409; **AB**). Gracia-Ibáñez et al. (J Biomech 2020, 109975; **EXC**) fit ellipses to the coupled angles used in 32 tasks by 18 adults and note that free circumduction reaches a high-flexion, ulnar-deviation corner that those tasks never use, and that gripping a cylinder shrinks it (citing Gehrmann).

Whether a swing uses the DTM plane is not stated by any baseball source I found; the golf data in A5 are in the anatomical axes, not in an oblique plane.

### A3. Wrist strength with a gripped handle

Note 9 has Holzbaur (5 men, isometric flexion 24–26, extension 13–14 N·m), Peleg (20 men, ulnar deviation 26.5, radial 14.9 N·m at 90°/s) and Kentel's tennis player. Added here:

- Ten men, 25.5 y (range 22–31), a force-sensor handle, forearm clamped, isometric 5 s, twice: **flexion 8.0 ± 3.0 N·m (forearm neutral), 8.3 ± 3.1 (pronated), 11.9 ± 2.9 (supinated); extension 4.6 ± 1.0, 6.5 ± 1.4, 5.5 ± 1.2** (Biomed Eng Online 2015;14:115, PMC4676844; **EXC**, first author not captured). Forearm position moves wrist strength by up to 50%; note 9's 24–26 N·m flexion came from a different method and the two sets disagree by a factor of two to three. I cannot say which is nearer to a hand on a bat.
- Grip strength depends on the wrist's angle: highest at slight extension (median **16.5°** over 9 subjects, range 0.5–50°; Ambike et al., PMC3766344, quasi-static, **EXC**); precision-grip force peaked at 30° of extension in 15 adults and fell 10–20% at 20–50° of flexion (Popp et al. 2023, J Neurophysiol; **EXC**); 37 tennis players' grip force at maximal wrist positions: neutral 80.2 ± 22.1 kg, extension 57.0 ± 18.4, **flexion 34.0 ± 9.5**, radial 56.3 ± 19.4, ulnar 56.6 ± 17.6 (ISBS conference record; **EXC**). A wrist forced into flexion keeps about 40% of its neutral-wrist grip.
- Athletes' wrist torques at speed: nothing beyond Laudner's untabulated 90 and 180°/s values (above).

### A4. Whether the bat moves in the hands

What the instrumented bats measured, and what they cannot say:

- **Koike, Kimura, Kawamura, Fujii, Takahashi & Ae, ISB 2003 long abstract (FT).** Five varsity players (68–79 kg, 1.69–1.82 m), tee batting at hip height, bat cut at the handle and rejoined by an aluminium bar with strain gauges that read the torsional moment about the grip axis between the hands, the bending moments and the axial forces; 500 Hz, Vicon at 120 Hz. The bottom (knob-side) hand does most of the accelerating; the top hand "modifies" and, just before impact, pushes the other way (its force along the bat turns negative). The two hands' forces form couples. The moment of the bottom hand about the bat's z-axis peaks about 140% through (of the phase times, where 100% is the downswing's start and 300% impact) and falls rapidly toward impact. **The plots carry the numbers; the abstract gives none, so I have no N·m**. The same bat in Koike's later work: Ae, Burke, Kawamura & Koike 2022 (J Appl Biomech 38:103; 23 collegiate players, tee, 28 strain gauges; **AB**) and Ae & Koike 2011 (ISBS 29; **AB**).
- **Miyanishi, ISBS 2009 (FT).** Eight varsity batters (19 ± 1 y, 1.71 m, 71 kg), 120 km/h machine, two high-speed cameras at 250 Hz, landmarks smoothed with quintic splines at **4–20 Hz** cut-offs; the grip's contributions to the bat head's 36.5 ± 2.9 m/s: the **force along the bat 54 ± 4%**, the **torque normal to the bat's axis 49 ± 13%** (the higher-speed half 52 ± 14, the lower 47 ± 13: "the snap of the grip" may differ), others at most 4%, the normal force's share −12 ± 9%. The torque here is the couple the two hands apply across the handle, which a rigid welded handle supplies without any grip slip; it does not say whether the handle turns in the palm.
- **Crisco, Osvalds & Rainbow, J Appl Biomech 2018;34:386 (AB).** 22 male players aged 13–18, 306 swings, three youth bats; inverse dynamics about the batter's hands from the bat's known motion: the force is mostly **along the bat away from the knob**, and **the moment on the bat is small until just before impact, then rises quickly**.
- **Finger pressures, Takahashi et al., Kanagawa Tai-iku Kenkyu 2011;44:11 (Japanese; EXC).** Six experienced players, tee, pressure sensors on gloves at 100 Hz: at impact the knob-side hand's ring finger carried the largest pressure; the barrel-side hand "hardly any" except one site; the grip diameter judged easiest to hold gave a lower impact pressure and less bat vibration. The authors read this as squeezing hard in the swing and not at impact.
- **Pressure over the palm.** Flynn et al., Orthop J Sports Med 2021;9(12) (PMC8671670; **TAB**): 14 NCAA D-I players, soft toss, 12 cameras at 200 Hz, **markers on the lower hand's metacarpal only**; peak pressure over the hook of the hamate, bare hand: 1.36 ± 0.73 kg/cm² in a full swing with all fingers on the bat, 1.68 ± 1.17 with one finger off, 1.18 ± 0.96 choked up, **3.72 ± 2.64 in a check swing with one finger off**. The same paper's wrist numbers are in A5.

**No source I found measured the handle turning, pivoting or sliding in either hand** (no motion of the bat relative to the hand's markers is reported by any of the above). The golf instrumented grips (A6) are the nearest. I list this under Not found.

### A5. Measured wrist and forearm angles through a baseball swing

These are for checking a built grip afterwards, not targets (PLAN D37). Every source's processing is stated.

**OpenBiomechanics Project, hitting (Driveline), computed by me from the open `hitting_joint_angles.zip` (release `dataset-v1`): 677 swings, 650 with the foot-plant and contact times.** Licence: cross-check only ([note 7](7-datasets-and-licences.md)). **mine** (scripts `obp_stats.py`, `obp_rng.py` in this session's scratchpad; to rerun them, download the zip). How it was made, from its README: markers at 360 Hz, angles by the right-hand rule, then "select adjustments to better match coach/player intuition (righty/lefty symmetry, sign negations, adding or subtracting 90°)"; filtering for the joint angles is [note 10](10-smoothed-sources.md)'s 20 Hz finding. The wrist's third angle (rotation) is **constrained**: the model's wrist has flexion and radial–ulnar deviation only; **forearm turn is in the elbow's third angle**, whose zero the README does not give. Hitters: mostly college, a few professionals. Foot plant is the lead foot at 100% of body weight (`fp_100`); contact is OBP's contact event. Wrist x: flexion (−) / extension (+); wrist y: ulnar (−) / radial (+).

Reading. **Lead wrist:** extended about 19° at foot plant, flexing to about 8° of flexion at contact (the mean traverse is about 27° of flexion), tilting from radial to **16° ulnar** (the "cock" releasing); the largest single-swing excursions in 0.5 s run to 46° (flexion/extension) and 33° (radial/ulnar) as medians. **Rear wrist:** **strongly extended through the swing, 37–77° on average, 47° at contact**: it hardly flexes (the range 33° is mostly extension coming back from about 77° early), and its radial–ulnar travel is smaller (about 20°). The forearm turns: rear forearm third angle changes **about 50°** between foot plant and contact (median), the lead about 20°. Single-swing maxima in the file reach 220° (rear wrist) and 188° (rear elbow third angle), which are wraps or tracking gaps; I report percentiles for that reason. Sign and zero conventions are the source's own and I have not checked them against a goniometer: the rear wrist's +47° extension at contact and the +77° early peak sit near the clinical extension limit of 70–76°, which is worth testing against the same source's marker set before trusting the early peak.

Golf has the wrist data baseball lacks and it agrees in direction with the baseball lead wrist (flexion and ulnar travel together, the trailing hand extended behind). It differs in the loads: a driver's 45 m/s head and a 0.3–0.4 kg club against a bat's 29–34 m/s sweet spot and 0.9 kg. Choi & Park's six-axis grip had the head-side hand (the trail hand) applying three times the grip-end hand's torque; Koike's baseball bat had the knob-side hand doing most of the accelerating and the top hand "modifying". Both are small samples; I read them as the two hands having different jobs, not as one hand's number carrying over to the other sport.

### A7. How stiff and how lossy is a grip, and how much torque can a hand hold about the handle

**Rotational stiffness and damping of a hand around a handle's own axis.**

- Schröder, Lindenmann & Matthiesen, Vibration 2023;6:23 (MDPI; **FT** excerpts): 13 adults, a shaker driving a cylindrical handle (taped for friction) around its axis, 10–100 Hz, grip forces 5.5–24 N and push forces 15–50 N: the response is **a spring and a damper**, with a phase near −π/4 from 16 to 80 Hz; the magnitude rises with grip force and is not changed by push force or posture. The magnitudes in N·m·s/rad are in the paper's figures; the excerpt has none. Spengler, Saurbier & Matthiesen (KIT data set, 2025; **AB**): 10 subjects, grip 70–135 N, push 25–65 N, 10–100 Hz, the impedance in N·m·s/rad and the apparent moment of inertia in kg·m² (the numbers are in a MATLAB file I did not open). What this cannot support: the motion is a vibration at 6 m/s², small and fast, so it measures skin and tissue; it says nothing about a handle rolling through tens of degrees in a palm.
- A hand–arm model's torsional spring–damper: an impact-wrench paper (ter Braack & Margolis, Machines 2026;14:213, **EXC**) tunes its hand's three rotational attachment frequencies to 1–25 Hz, 30–42 Hz and 60–132 Hz by axis, a design choice and not a measurement.
- Stiffness of the **wrist** inside its range: [note 9](9-arm-limits.md) has Formica (0.6–1.9 N·m/rad) and Crisco's ellipse above (0.15–0.4 N·m/°, which is 8.6–23 N·m/rad). The two differ by a factor of 10–15; Crisco's is the cadaver wrist at ±2 N·m, Formica's the living one.

The reading: **a bare hand holds on the order of 3–8 N·m about a handle's own axis before it slips**, depending on diameter, grip force and surface, and the best case (45–50 mm, forearm free) is about 8 N·m; the slip limit is friction × squeeze × the handle's radius, T = μ·G·r, so a harder squeeze raises it in proportion (for a 33 mm bat handle, a 500 N squeeze and the 0.85–1.43 of Seo's two surfaces, the formula would give 7–12 N·m with a uniform pressure; this is arithmetic on the cited μ and not a measurement). None of these tasks was a swing: the load arrives in milliseconds with the hand's tissue already deformed.

**Torque across the handle (about its two perpendicular axes).** The bat's bending moment between the hands is supplied by the couple of the two hands' forces, so it is a force problem and not a grip-slip problem; Koike's and Miyanishi's couples (A4) are the evidence and the numbers sit in plots. Crisco 2018 (youth bats): moments small until just before impact. **No number for the torque a hand can hold across a handle was found.**

# Body research 12: the trunk's bending, and the legs and feet turning under load (2026-11-25, worker body-research-2)

For [PLAN D41](../../../PLAN.md) and the spec's §4.10 (on the branch `work/body-3d`): the body's trunk has no muscles for bending forward, back or sideways, and its knees and feet cannot turn. Whether that is what keeps a swing from reaching the ball is **a thing to test, not a finding** (the Coordinator's correction, 2026-11-25). This note reports what sources measured. It argues for no number. It extends [note 8](8-strength-at-speed.md) (the trunk's twist) and [note 5](5-strength-limits.md) (the ankle's push-off and the knee's strength).

**Reading status:** **FT** full text read; **TAB** a table of an open paper read; **AB** abstract only; **EXC** the passage a search tool returned, page not opened further; **2nd** quoted by another source; **mine** computed by me from a source's open data or converted from its units (1 ft·lb = 1.3558 N·m; 1°/s = 0.01745 rad/s). SI beside original units. Two search workers read most of the sources in C and D under these rules; I computed the OpenBiomechanics values myself (my numbers and one worker's agree to the first decimal). A source I reached only as an abstract says so.

## Part C. The trunk's bending

### C1. Strength in N·m: bending forward, back and to each side

The trunk's **flexion** is bending forward, **extension** bending back, **lateral flexion** (or side bend) to the side. "About L3" or "about L5–S1" says where the moment is taken.

| Source | Subjects, posture, device | Extension | Flexion | Side bend | Read |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |

**Where they disagree.** Men's extension at 60°/s runs **145 to 440 N·m** (a factor of three) and the flexion/extension order differs by source (Unver: flexion above extension; Thompson standing: about equal; Zouita, Dervisevic, Baur: extension above flexion). The same torque is measured about different axes, in standing, seated and lying postures with the pelvis restrained differently (the 2020 review says flexion is larger standing than seated, with extension unchanged). Zouita's 2018 and 2019 papers print the same athlete and control numbers under different group sizes and speeds, so the 440 N·m is **one data point of doubtful provenance, not two**; Zouita's introduction attributes "208 N·m (121–360) extension, 176 (111–296) flexion" to Thompson 1985, and I could not find those numbers in Thompson. Speed: the standing sample loses about 9% of extension from 30 to 120°/s (0.5 to 2.1 rad/s) and the seated one about 8% from 60 to 120°/s; nothing above 3.1 rad/s was found for flexion, extension or side bend.

**Athletes.** Wrestlers and tennis players, isometric by load cell, in **newtons with no lever arm** (so no N·m): wrestlers flexion 852 N, extension 951 N, side bends 759 and 744 N; tennis players extension 835, flexion 638, left side bend 643 N against right 557 (a 1990 Am J Sports Med paper; **AB**). Professional pitchers (n = 25, 24.5 y, 73.6 kg; Jin 1998, **AB**): Cybex TEF, standing; the flexion/extension torque ratio 82%, 90% and 95% at 30, 60 and 120°/s, and trunk flexor torque correlated with throwing speed; no N·m. Andersson, Swärd & Thorstensson 1988 (Med Sci Sports Exerc 20:587; 57 elite male athletes, Cybex II at 15 and 30°/s, lying, pivot at L2–L3 or the hip; **AB**): all athlete groups above 87 conscripts, the largest differences in hip extension and trunk flexion, the smallest in trunk extension, and wrestlers and tennis players stronger toward the non-dominant side in lateral flexion; **the N·m are in tables I did not reach**. Sumimoto 2023 (5 Japanese professional pitchers; EXC): trunk lateral flexion range 35 ± 5.0° and 35 ± 9.4°, falling to 22–25° after the season, strength in newtons by hand-held device. **No N·m for side bend or for flexion or extension was found for baseball players, golfers, hockey players or rowers**; the 2020 review says none of its 42 studies measured isokinetic lateral flexion.

What these cannot support: any speed in a swing (the fastest set is 2.1 rad/s for flexion and extension and 1.05 for side bend), hitters, or a moment about the joint where a swing's trunk bends (the hip and the lumbar spine each move the trunk; see C2).

### C2. Where the bending is

Frames differ by source: **hip** is the thigh against the pelvis, **pelvis** the pelvis's tilt in space, **lumbar** the pelvis against the thorax, **thoracic** the upper back's own bend; they are not interchangeable.

**Bending forward** (maximal, standing, slow):

| Source | Subjects | Hip / pelvis | Lumbar | Thoracic | Read |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Esola et al., Spine 1996 | 41 adults (21 without back pain), 3D optical | **69.4°** | **41.6°** (total 111°) | — | **AB** |
| Tafazzol et al., Clin Biomech 2014 | 8 asymptomatic men, inertial sensors | pelvis 53.0 ± 5.2° | 60.2 ± 8.6° (thorax 121 ± 9.9° against the lab) | included in "lumbar" | **AB** |
| Hirata et al. 1998 | 20 young adults, electromagnetic | pelvis 53.4 ± 15.3° | 59.1 ± 10.8° | — | **EXC** |
| PMC2200722, Table 1 | 20 young men | hip 69.2 ± 10.3° | 55.9 ± 5.8° | — | **TAB** |
| Mousavi et al. 2018 (PMC6193559), Table 1 | 19 adults (8 women), mean age 47, markers on the spine | pelvis 51.8 ± 15.1° | 47.4 ± 15.6° | 7.1 ± 11.8° | **TAB** |
| Narimani & Arjmand 2018 | 22 adults, inertial sensors | pelvis 42.8 ± 21.6° | 55.4 ± 12.4° | 19.9 ± 6.4° | **AB** |

The lumbar spine moves first and the hips last: lumbar-to-hip ratios of 1.9, 0.9, 0.4 across the thirds of the first 90° (Esola), 2.5 falling to 1.3 (Tafazzol), the lumbar share of the total 41.7 ± 7.1% in normally flexible students and 49.7 ± 10.4% in the stiff (Takahashi & Yamaji 2020, PMC7064356, **TAB**), 78.7% of the first quarter falling to 15.9% of the last. The sources define "pelvic motion" either against the thigh or against gravity, and "lumbar" as the thorax against the pelvis (so thoracic bending is inside it in Tafazzol); the hip's share of the total forward bend is 46–62% across them.

**Bending back:** lumbar 10.9 ± 10.9° (Mousavi) to 23.4 ± 10.1° (Narimani); pelvis 12.5 ± 6.1° and 10.2 ± 6.4°; thoracic 9.5 ± 14.2° and 11.7 ± 3.4° (same samples; **TAB**, **AB**).

**Bending sideways, each side:** Narimani, left/right: thoracic **24.5 ± 7.4° / 26.5 ± 6.1°**, lumbar 16.4 ± 7.2° / 18.3 ± 5.7°, pelvis 11.0 ± 4.4° / 9.3 ± 6.2° (**AB**). Mousavi, total left to right: thoracic 56.2 ± 16.3°, lumbar 28.6 ± 10.9°, pelvis 26.5 ± 8.8° (**TAB**); the authors say the thoracic segment accounts for about half. Kansas thesis (Cadel 2015; adolescents and older adults, not athletes; **EXC**): thoracic is about 60% of the torso's lateral bend. Radiographic lumbar, per level (Pearcy 1984/1985, three groups of 10–11 healthy men aged 24–29, pelvis restrained, standing; the text of the 1985 compilation, tables unreadable): lateral bending about **10° at each of L1–2, L2–3, L3–4, 6° at L4–5 and 3° at L5–S1** (39° if those are one side and 78° if both; the text does not say, and "no difference between right and left" suggests one side; unconfirmed). Flexion plus extension about 14° per level, three quarters of it flexion. Troke et al. 2001/2005 (405 asymptomatic adults, 209 men, 16–90 y, lumbar by a CA6000 analyser; **AB** and a secondary table): lateral flexion 28° falling to 14° each side. Fluoroscopy in 8 asymptomatic adults doing a functional bend (PMC2899583; **TAB**): left–right bending 2.9 ± 2.4° (L2–3), 3.4 ± 2.1°, 4.7 ± 2.4° (L4–5) per level, much smaller than Pearcy's end-range numbers because the task was not an end range. A secondary table of White & Panjabi gives thoracic lateral inclination 36°, flexion plus extension 62°; a 2025 review says White & Panjabi tend to run up to three times the in-vivo values for thoracic lateral bending and twist.

What these cannot support: how the shares change at a swing's speed (every task above was slow, self-paced or held), or a young male athlete's own split (the samples are mixed-sex and mean-age 22–47).

### C3. For checking afterward: how far hitters tilt and bend the trunk

Not targets. Processing stated.

**OpenBiomechanics Project (Driveline), hitting, computed by me from `hitting_joint_angles.zip`** (677 swings from 98 hitters, mostly college; against a machine at about 65 mph from 40 ft; marker data at 360 Hz, 4th-order Butterworth low-pass at **20 Hz**; the frame and Euler sequence of the torso and pelvis angles are **not stated** in the README; the axial angle is about −94° at foot plant, so "x" and "y" are only approximately sagittal and frontal tilts). **mine**; licence is cross-check only. Foot plant = the lead foot at 100% of body weight; contact = OBP's estimate. Mean ± SD:

| Torso angle (°) | foot contact (10% BW) | **foot plant** | **contact** |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| forward tilt (x; flexion −, extension +) | −27.1 ± 10.2 | **−26.5 ± 9.5** (flexed) | **+15.0 ± 7.5** (extended) |
| side tilt toward the rear leg (y; toward +) | −5.6 ± 9.2 | **+4.1 ± 9.0** (range −16.8 to 40.7) | **+32.2 ± 7.5** (13.2 to 55.8) |
| pelvis forward tilt (x) | −22.7 ± 7.9 | −22.1 ± 8.3 | +7.0 ± 6.4 |
| pelvis side tilt (y) | +4.3 ± 4.7 | +4.9 ± 4.2 | +8.9 ± 4.7 |
| torso minus pelvis, side tilt | — | +7.3 ± 6.2 | **+23.2 ± 6.1** |

So the torso goes from about 27° flexed at foot plant to about 15° extended at contact (a change of about 41°), and tilts 28° more toward the rear side. The range from foot plant to contact (median [p10–p90] per swing): forward tilt **43.9° [30.6–52.5]**, side tilt **29.1° [15.7–43.6]**. The project's earlier summary (note 6) has 7.2° at foot plant for the side tilt from the dataset's own point-of-interest file; my full-signal value is 4.1° (the difference is the event time; not resolved). The "torso" is one rigid thorax segment from skin markers: it does not split the lumbar from the thoracic spine, so C2 cannot be read from it.

## Part D. The legs and feet turning under load

### D1. The ankle with weight on it

**The weight-bearing lunge (the knee-to-wall test):** the shin's lean over the foot with the heel down.

**The ankle's other motions** (goniometer, mostly not weight-bearing): 987 young adults, 513 men (Hallaceli et al. 2014, Acta Orthop Traumatol Turc 48:37; **TAB**): passive dorsiflexion **22.4 ± 7.1°**, passive plantarflexion **50.0 ± 9.3°**, passive inversion **33–34 ± 10°**, passive eversion **19.9 ± 6.2°** (men's 34.6, 19.1). Roaas & Andersson 1982 (Acta Orthop Scand 53:205; 105 men, 30–40 y, FT Table 1; also in the spec): dorsiflexion 15.3 ± 5.8°, plantarflexion 39.7 ± 7.5°, eversion 27.6 ± 4.6°, inversion 27.7 ± 6.9° (the AAOS column printed beside them: 18, 48, 18, 33). Three samples, three plantarflexions (39.7°, 45–50°, 57°).

**The subtalar joint** (the roll that turns the sole in or out), with and without weight: Lattanza, Gray & Kantner, JOSPT 1988;9:310 (FT; 17 therapists and students, 15 women): the calcaneus's eversion **6.6° passive without weight (3–10°) and 10.4° standing on a board with a 10 lb weight (5–16°)**, 37% more with weight; Yamaguchi et al. 2008 (7 healthy adults, fluoroscopy; FT of the abstract): with weight the subtalar joint was 7–8° more everted. Yang et al. 2020 (PMC7878402; 20 adults, biplane radiography, walking; **TAB**): subtalar inversion/eversion through a stance **9.4–10.5°**, tibiotalar rotation 5.9–6.9°. Reviews quote 25–30° of subtalar inversion and 5–10° of eversion (2nd), and two methods on the same people differ by 11° in inversion. Total inversion–eversion at **4 N·m**: 34.1 ± 10.1° in 50 men and 42.1 ± 9.0° in 50 women with an arthrometer (PMC3070499; **AB**); 66.2° in a 100-subject thesis at the same torque (different axis and device).

### D2. The toes

How far the big toe's joint at the ball of the foot (the first metatarsophalangeal joint) bends back:

- **Passive range:** about **57–82°** by source: Nawoczenski, Baumhauer & Umberger, JBJS-A 1999;81:370 (33 subjects, electromagnetic tracking; **AB**; the passive value 57° is **2nd**), Joseph 73°, Shereff 76°, Buell et al. 1988 **82°** (2nd). A hallux-rigidus-patient study has 45.1 ± 10.7° passive (48 patients; not a healthy range).
- **Weight-bearing, active:** **44°** (Nawoczenski, AB); the same paper has a **heel-rise** test at **58°**; walking used **42°**. Hopson, McPoil & Cornwall 1995 (20 adults, video; **AB**): about **65°** needed for walking. A review (2nd) says 65–75° for propulsion and only 25–30° if the first ray cannot plantarflex. **Needed in walking: 42° against 65° by method; 25° by another (OA patients, walking maximum 25.4 ± 6.7°; Allan 2020, FT)**.
- **Sprinting:** the shoe's bend at the ball of the foot reached **36.5° (men) and 37.7° (women)** in 70 Olympic 100 m runners at about 60 m (Stefanyshyn, Krell & Chow 2002; 2 cameras at 120 Hz, hand-digitised; a shoe-sole angle, not a bone angle; **AB**); the joint's peak plantarflexor **moment 59.9 N·m in running and 112.4 N·m in sprinting** in 10 trained men (Stefanyshyn & Nigg 1997; sagittal inverse dynamics; **AB**); in sprinters the heel rises from about 40 to 110 ms of stance while the joint dorsiflexes (Smith et al. 2012, 8 sprinters; filtering at 8 Hz under-read the range by about 15° against 100 Hz; **AB**). Spikes cut the joint's range by 17.9% (8 sprinters, JAB; **AB**).
- **In a baseball swing:** no source gave the toe's bend or the heel's height. The rear heel's release in one analyst's video of 50 hitters (Farnsworth, FanGraphs 2013; **EXC**, unvalidated) follows the front foot's contact and the start of the hips' turn. **Not found**: the windlass in a swing, athletes' weight-bearing values.

With weight on it: **the range falls about 58–60% in living knees loaded by 40% of body weight through the foot** (Shultz 2007, EXC) and **80% under "physiological compression"** (Freeman & Pinskerova, 2nd), against 20–25% in other cadaver reports. I found no measured absolute range for a planted foot under body weight at stated flexion angles. In weight-bearing movements the knee's turn is small: tibial IR 6.1 ± 7.6° from straight to 30° of flexion in a single-leg lunge (7 subjects, fluoroscopy; Qi 2013; **TAB**) and 4.9° at 50° of flexion in a step-up, with the foot placed in maximal ER giving 9.0° of tibial ER there (Kärrholm 2000; **EXC**), i.e. a foot's position carries into the tibia; the knee does not absorb it. **Strength of tibial rotation in N·m: not found for healthy men** (Hester & Falkel 1984 was behind a 403; Armour 2004's 30 reconstructed knees have the uninjured side's internal rotation 27.8 N·m at 60°/s, 21.6 at 120°/s, 18.2 at 180°/s: ft·lb converted by me, patients).

### D4. The back foot and the front foot in a swing

**No study reporting the rear foot's yaw (its pivot angle in the lab) or its heel's lift against time in a baseball swing was found.** What there is:

- **Front foot, Welch et al. 1995 (FT):** the foot's line from ankle to forefoot against the line to the pitcher was **67° "closed" at forefoot contact and 61 ± 11° at the heel's contact**; stride 85 ± 12 cm, the stride's direction 12 ± 3° closed. Whether the 6° is turning or the marker's rise is not stated. Rear foot: no angle; the right knee 32° at foot off and 45° at contact; forces: rear foot **102% of body weight at foot off, 58% at foot down, 16% at contact**, front foot 123% at foot down and 84% at contact (the latter figures via a secondary source). Hips' fastest turn 714°/s 75 ms before contact. Processing: markers on the shoe's top, 13.3 Hz.
- **OpenBiomechanics ankle angles (mine, from the joint-angle file; the model has no toe or heel marker, and the ankle's "rotation" is the foot's angle against the shank, not in the lab).** Ankle dorsi(+)/plantar(−) flexion: rear **97.5 ± 10.5° at foot plant and 79.1 ± 10.9° at contact** (about 18° more toward plantarflexion, "rear heel coming up"; the zero is not stated and values near 90° suggest an offset), lead 86.4 ± 8.4° → 65.3 ± 8.4°. Ankle eversion(+)/inversion(−): rear −24.4 ± 10.2° → −14.8 ± 14.7° (range from plant to contact median **19.3° [6.2–32.4]**), lead −13.1 ± 10.7° → +17.3 ± 10.9°. Foot's rotation against the shank (lateral +/medial −): rear −10.5 ± 7.7° → −12.0 ± 9.6° (median range **8.4° [4.0–15.8]**), lead −5.1 ± 8.2° → −9.2 ± 11.0°. **The rear hip's rotation** (internal +/external −): **+21.7 ± 11.6° at plant → −14.3 ± 9.4° at contact** (a range of **36.8° [23.0–50.3]** per swing; lead −0.0 ± 11.0° → +8.5 ± 12.1°). I did not check whether the foot turned in the lab. **mine**; the sign and zero conventions are the dataset's and unchecked; filtered at 20 Hz.
- **Ground forces:** Ae, Koike, Fujii & Ae 2017 (Sports Biomech 16:283; 22 collegiate men, tee, three plates; **AB**): the medial–lateral forces of both legs made the whole-body moment about the vertical, the lead leg's twice the pivot leg's, and the pivot-side hip's external rotation larger in the high-moment group (d = 1.02). **No free moment (the twisting moment of the ground on a foot) and no N·m** in any baseball source I reached.
- **Golf** has more foot data: the lead foot toed out **14.96 ± 5.84°** at address (13 professionals, FT; Kim et al. 2022, PMC9805371), 20–30° (2nd); it rotates through the swing by **43.3° (no back pain) to 48.8°** from address to the follow-through in 12 amateurs (McRae thesis, **FT** excerpt); the Roehampton study (8 PGA coaches, 5-iron, Plug-in-Gait): trail "ankle rotation" −8.5 ± 6.2° at address, +16.2 ± 7.3° at the top, **−27.5 ± 5.7° at impact** and lead −13.7, −32.6, −0.6° (the sign is not defined in the excerpt and the angle is the shank–foot angle). Free moment about the vertical, both feet on one plate: **0.83, 1.00, 1.19 N·m/kg in easy, medium and hard swings of 10 professionals**, peaking just after the maximum trunk twist (Meister et al. 2011, J Appl Biomech 27:242; **FT**; that is about 102 N·m for the 85.9 kg mean at the hard swing, my multiplication, and it includes the two feet's horizontal-force couple). Han et al. 2019 (63 golfers; **AB**) and Bourgain 2017 (6 golfers; **EXC**): the torque of each foot's twist alone is "the smallest" and "negligible" beside the other terms, against a vendor's white paper (20–25%, **EXC**); the definitions differ. **Cricket**: no number.

**Torque rises with load but not in proportion** (Serensits: 8.4 N·m over 534 N, a slope of 0.016 N·m per N with an intercept; torque divided by load fell from 0.060 m to 0.042 m; **mine**). Kent's rig at 2.8 kN gave a ratio of 0.043–0.062 m on natural grass (**mine**). A tibia–ankle's own limit: about **75 N·m under 1,000 N** from Hirsch & Lewis's cadavers (2nd via Serensits) and a "theoretical safe torque" of about 95 N·m at 1,000 N (Villwock, cut off in my excerpt); the living ankle's torsional stiffness about 1.2 N·m/° against the rig's rubber washer at 11 N·m/° (2nd). Injury rates rose from 4.2 to 19.2 per 1,000 exposures as the peak torque went from 15–31 to 39–55 N·m in 555 high-school players at 580 N (Wannop, via Thomson; 2nd). **Disagreement the sources explain but do not settle:** baseball dirt at the same shoe differed 30–35% between two sampling dates (the paper does not say why); a different device at 45 kg gave about 24 N·m on a similar basepath (Goodall 2005, 2nd via Brosnan). **There is no dimensionless rotational friction coefficient for a cleat on dirt in any open source I read** (Torg's release coefficient of 0.29–0.41 on artificial turf is 2nd with no lever arm); no values for wet dirt, a batter's box of clay, or a mound.

What these cannot support: a hitter's own foot twisting (every number is a rig's peak at a stated load and angle), the load on the rear foot in a swing (58% of body weight at foot down and 16% at contact in Welch's seven, which is well under the rigs' loads), and the batter's box.

## What this gives the design

1. **Trunk bending strength has only slow, mixed-posture numbers**: 164–204 N·m isometric standing (10 men, about L3), 250 N·m or more isokinetic standing, 140–440 N·m extension seated. Nothing was found for side bend in athletes in N·m, and nothing above 2 rad/s.
2. **The bending is split**: in forward bend the hips take 46–62% and the lumbar spine 41–60° of about 110°, the upper back 7–20°; in side bend each side the upper back takes about 25°, the lumbar 16–18° and the pelvis 9–11° (Narimani), with one radiographic series of lumbar levels.
3. **Hitters' trunks** (OBP, 20 Hz): about 27° flexed at foot plant, 15° extended at contact; side tilt 4° at plant and 32° at contact (23° of it trunk on pelvis); Welch and Fortenbaugh give less, with the lean split between pelvis and trunk three times differently. All for checking.
4. **The rear foot's turn and heel lift are not measured in any source reached**; the nearest are OBP's ankle and rear-hip angles (the rear hip goes from +22° to −14° of rotation between plant and contact; the rear ankle 18° toward plantarflexion) and golf's foot angles. The knee turns 35–44° (active, women) to 57° (men, boot) and, under load, 58–80% less; the toe bends 42–82° by method; the ankle's lean with weight on is 25° (knee straight) or 39° (knee bent).
5. **What a cleat resists with**: 68–100 N·m for a baseball shoe on basepath dirt at about 1.2 kN, 120–174 N·m at 2.8 kN for football cleats on grass, 21–55 N·m at 450–1,300 N; it rises with load and none is a player's own twist.
6. **What was not found is as much as what was**: free moment per foot in baseball, rear-foot yaw, tibial-rotation strength, a dirt friction coefficient.

## Not found

- **C:** trunk side-bend strength (N·m) for baseball players, golfers, hockey players or rowers; Andersson 1988's tables; Kumar 1995, Beimborn & Morrissey 1988 and McNeill 1980 numbers; flexion, extension or side-bend strength above 2.1 rad/s; the regional (lumbar against thoracic) split of backward bend from radiographs; Welch's Table 3; Szymanski, Inkster, Katsumata and Escamilla 2009 trunk tilt; the OBP torso's Euler sequence.
- **D:** the rear foot's yaw and heel lift in a measured swing (baseball or cricket), and per-foot free moment in baseball; trail heel lift in golf; Livesay 2006's numbers; Hopson's and Nawoczenski's passive weight-bearing first-joint values; isometric ankle inversion/eversion in N·m for healthy men; voluntary tibial-rotation torque in N·m for healthy men (Hester & Falkel 1984, Osternig 1980); one study's torque–angle curve for the living knee at 20, 45 and 90°; the tibial turn's range under body weight at stated angles; a rotational friction coefficient for a cleat on dirt or clay, wet dirt, a batter's box or a mound; Torg's release-coefficient table.

## Searches that came up empty

- Lateral-flexion peak torque in baseball, golf, hockey or rowing: only twist studies and flexion/extension reviews; the 2020 review says none of 42 papers measured lateral flexion.
- Andersson 1988's numbers, Esola's and Tafazzol's full texts, Pearcy's tables 4–5: abstracts, method text or garbled tables only.
- Baseball rear-foot pivot angle or heel lift; Katsumata, Szymanski, Inkster foot data; cricket back-foot rotation; golf trail heel lift in centimetres: no numeric source.
- OpenBiomechanics landmarks and README for toe, heel or free-moment columns: none (the ankle joint centres and ankle angle components only).
- Livesay 2006 and Hester & Falkel 1984 (a 403), Han & Kwon 2019, Barrentine 1994, Markolf tables, Nawoczenski 1999 full text: paywalled or blocked.
- Isometric inversion/eversion torque in N·m for men: hand-held papers give kgf.

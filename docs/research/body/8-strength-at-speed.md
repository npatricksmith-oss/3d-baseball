# Body research 8: trunk strength, strength at speed and elastic stretch (2026-11-25, worker strength-research)

For the owner's choice in [body-motion §4.4](../../body-motion.md): a higher trunk bound or a flatter force–velocity law for professionals (option b), or an elastic part (option c). This note extends [note 5](5-strength-limits.md) and reports what the sources support; it argues for no option. Reading status: **FT** full text read; **TAB** a table read from an open full text; **AB** abstract only; **EXC** the passage a search tool returned from the page, page not opened further (tables may be garbled); **2nd** a number quoted by another paper or thesis. SI beside the original units. "My arithmetic" marks anything I computed.

**The question in numbers.** The 3D inverse dynamics of the mean hitter (92 kg, 1.84 m, from Fortenbaugh 2011) needs trunk rotation of **330–440 N·m** (344 on the rate-curve reading) against a bound of **288**, i.e. 1.15–1.53 × (my arithmetic). It needs the rear hip's extension (256 N·m at 3.3 rad/s), the lead knee's extension (250 at 2.5), the lead hip's adduction (123–134 at 7.1) and both hips' rotators to give 70–95% of full strength at 2.5–7 rad/s.

**A correction to note 5.** Note 5 §1 says Anderson 2007's dynamometer range "was about ≤ 3–4 rad/s". The paper (PMC6820133, **TAB/EXC**) also records **four "high speed" concentric maximal efforts per exertion at the Biodex's top speed, 500°/s (8.7 rad/s)**, "effectively minimizing resistance", with the subject told to move as fast as possible, besides isokinetic trials at 60 and 120°/s (hip, ankle) or 75 and 150°/s (knee). I did not read how those efforts entered the fit of C4 and C5. The law is therefore not purely an extrapolation above 4 rad/s for Anderson's recreational adults, but what the 8.7 rad/s trials contributed is unread.

What this says: professional rotational athletes' peak trunk rotation torque on a seated machine is **about 130–180 N·m for a 92 kg man at ≤ 2 rad/s, about 250–290 N·m at mean + 2 SD.** Golfers are **not** much stronger than non-golfers (Bae: significant only at one speed; Gifford: no differences; the 2006 paper: no differences). I found **no baseball, cricket, hockey or throwing sample with trunk rotation torque in N·m**. I could not reproduce the spec's 288 from Bae alone (I get 260 with weight × height scaling, 249 with mass only); the 288 may come from another golf set.

**Where 330–440 N·m sits.** In Bae's golfers 330 N·m for 92 kg is a 257 N·m torque at their size, **3.8 SD above their 30°/s mean**; 440 N·m is **6.5 SD** (my arithmetic, same scaling). It is **outside** every measured seated value I found. Two reservations the sources raise: (1) the machine fixes the pelvis and legs and tests only the trunk's own muscles in a seated, arms-crossed position, whereas in a swing the trunk muscles act with the legs braced, the pelvis moving, the arms attached and inertial loads on them; no measurement links the two; (2) inverse dynamics reports the *net* torque at the joint, which includes passive tissue and the contribution of two-joint muscles, not only the contractile torque a machine isolates.

The hyperbola of the 2005 paper, as I read it (T = T0 ω_c (ω_max − ω)/(ω_max (ω + ω_c)), my reconstruction from the equation's description since the page text is garbled), gives for Wilson's hip extension **0.38 of T0 at 3.3 rad/s and 0.22 at 5.6**; for the knee extension **0.73 at 2.5 and 0.49 at 5.24** (the differential-activation factor, which lowers the curve at low concentric speeds, is left out). So the one elite-athlete hip curve in the literature I found is **steeper** than the spec's law at the hip speeds in question, not flatter, while its isometric torque (480 N·m, subject mass not read) is **above the spec's 348 bound**. That is a single jumper and a Hill-type shape fitted to 50–450°/s crank speeds, and the paper warns that it is not the same as the joint's speed (below).

**What isokinetic data at 3–9 rad/s cannot say.** King & Yeadon 2002 (J Appl Biomech 18:207, **AB**): the dynamometer crank and the joint differed by **up to 35°** during isovelocity trials, and faster crank speeds gave smaller isovelocity ranges; the simulated series elastic element allowed the joint velocity to stand for the contractile velocity at 150°/s "during most of the isovelocity trial". Torque at high crank speeds is therefore measured over a short window after a ramp, and Yeadon & King say faster data would remove the need to rescale. Bae set 120°/s as the limit for trustworthy trunk tests.

## 3. Stretch before shortening

### 3.1 How much, and how fast it decays

| Source | Subjects, task | Result | Read |

At the swing's own timing: the hips lead the trunk, the stretch builds from stride foot contact (~−150 to −100 ms) and the trunk shortens within about 100–200 ms. With the exponential of Wilson 1991 applied to a 100 ms delay, the benefit would be reduced by **8%** (2^(−0.1/0.85), my arithmetic); the sources do not say it applies to the trunk, to a rotation or to a delay this short, and the one muscle with a delay series (Fortuna 2017) shows work falling as the delay rises (the amounts not read).

**What is not a number.** Walshe et al. and Fukutani et al. find that **much of the gain is an active state built before the movement starts, not recoil**. In a model, a muscle that is already activated and loaded at the start of shortening (a rise from the earlier hip drive, an isometric start of the trunk muscles) gets most of this without an elastic element.

None of these is a trunk or swing measurement. What they give: **biarticular muscles can carry on the order of 7–31% of a joint's work in explosive leg extensions** (Hill-model estimates), and a model that ignores them can understate performance by 4–14 percentage points of jump height (Lewis et al.). For the swing the pairs that matter (hamstrings and gluteus maximus at rear hip and knee; rectus femoris and iliopsoas at the lead side; latissimus dorsi and the obliques across trunk, pelvis and arm) have **no number** in anything I opened. Passive end-range torque at the hips has no value; only the finding that the hips reach their passive range in the swing.

**What the hitting inverse-dynamics papers give for the legs, for scale.** Horiuchi, Nakashima & Sakurai, Taiikugaku kenkyu 2018 (98 male amateurs, 172.6 ± 5.6 cm, 70.3 ± 8.1 kg, tee, 250 Hz, force plates; **EXC**, table garbled; the signs below are as I could read them): peak torque on the pitcher (lead) side flexion **104.3 ± 27.5 N·m**, extension **160.7 ± 42.3**, adduction **158.4 ± 31.6**, internal rotation **47.2 ± 23.2**; catcher (rear) side extension **210.7 ± 39.3**, adduction **84.4 ± 20.7**, external rotation **62.8 ± 24.7**. Rear extension and lead extension correlated with bat-head speed (r = −0.57, −0.49 as printed, the sign is the torque's). Scaled to 92 kg, 1.84 m by weight × height (× 1.395, my arithmetic): rear extension **294**, lead adduction **221**, lead flexion **145**, rear external rotation **88**, lead extension **224** N·m. The spec's needs for the mean professional are rear extension 256, lead adduction 123–134, lead flexion 114: **at or below what an amateur's swing is reported to need**, with the caveats that the hip axes differ (Euler angles of the thigh relative to the lower torso, not the thigh's own three axes), that these are net torques at whatever speeds the hips turn at (not given in my excerpt), and that they are tee swings of amateurs. Ae et al. 2017 (Sports Biomech 16, 22 collegiate players, tee, **AB**): hip flexion/extension, stride-hip adduction and stride-knee extension torques gave the largest mechanical work.

## 5. What this gives the decision

### Shortfall 1: trunk rotation, 330–440 N·m against 288

- **A bound.** The measured means for professional rotational athletes on a seated machine are **130–180 N·m for 92 kg (≤ 2 rad/s); mean + 2 SD is 250–290** (Bae 260, Tsai 184, Gifford ≈ 270, tennis men scaled 175 as a mean). **288 is the top of what the sources give; 330–440 N·m is 3.8–6.5 SD above Bae's golfers' mean.** No source supports 330 or more as a *voluntary maximum*, in any posture. A range a source would carry: **bound 250–290 N·m** (mean + 2 SD) for a professional of this size; if the owner takes a stated assumption above that, the nearest evidence is the gap between a seated, pelvis-fixed test and a standing swing, which no source measures. The golfers' tests show no skill effect on peak torque, so a "professional hitter" premium is unsupported.
- **A law.** For the trunk, the measured fall is **flatter than the spec's** law: **83–84% kept at 2.1 rad/s** (professional golfers), **82% at 4.2 rad/s** (Gifford), **90–92% at 2.1 rad/s** (tennis, one doubling of speed) and 96–99% at 2.1 rad/s (Tsai), against the spec's **83% at 1.0 rad/s and 50% at 3.3**. A law keeping 80–90% to 4 rad/s is what the trunk data support. The relative trunk speed in the swing is about 1 rad/s at the peak need (spec table: 239 N·m allowed at 1.0 rad/s), so a flatter trunk law raises the allowed 239 to about 245–275 N·m and does **not** close the gap to 330–440.
- **A caution on the number itself.** The only trunk-torque inverse-dynamics figures found (golf lumbar axial torque, one thesis table, about 71 N·m for 92 kg) are 4–6 times lower, and a second thesis shows the same swing giving 25–444 N·m depending on the root and chain; so the 330–440 N·m rests on the reconstruction of the upper body, as §4.4 already says, and no independent source gives a trunk torque in a baseball swing in N·m.

### Shortfall 2: hips and lead knee at 2.5–7 rad/s

- **A law.** Measured slow-to-fast retention in athletes: **knee extension 52–55% of the 1.05 rad/s value at 5.24 rad/s** (about 0.45 of isometric if 0.83 is the isometric-to-1.05 fraction, my arithmetic); **hip extension 79–84% of the 1.05 rad/s value at 3.1–4.2 rad/s** (about 0.65–0.70 of isometric, same assumption). The need is **70–95% of full strength at 2.5–7 rad/s**; the sources support the *lower end* for the hip extensors out to 4 rad/s, **do not reach** 70% at 5 rad/s or more for the knee, and have **no value at all** for hip adduction, abduction or rotation, or for any joint at 7 rad/s (only an unreported 350°/s test, Hegyi). The one elite curve from a torque-driven model (Wilson 2006's hip, ω_max 14.2, ω_c 3.2) is **steeper** than the spec's. A plausible band for the law is therefore no flatter than "75% at 1.6 and 50% at 3.2 rad/s for hip extension (Anderson), up to about 0.65–0.70 of isometric at 4 rad/s (Silva, Rodrigues)", with **unsourced extrapolation above 4–5 rad/s**.
- **A bound.** Where the bound, not the law, may be low: an elite jumper's isometric torques (480 N·m hip extension, 328 N·m knee extension, Wilson 2006) exceed the spec's 348 and 352; and amateurs' reported hip torques in the swing (Horiuchi 2018, scaled: rear extension 294, lead adduction 221) are above the hips' needs in §4.4. A bound 1.2–1.4 × higher than the spec's is therefore not excluded by anything found; I found no source that excludes it or fixes it.
- **Net joint torque is not contractile torque.** Inverse dynamics counts passive structures and two-joint action; the machines count single-joint voluntary effort. The gaps between them are not measured for the swing.

### Elastic stretch (option c)

- **Size.** What the sources measure for a muscle group giving more after a stretch: **work in the shortening phase +8% to +18% (voluntary knee, 25 people), +9% to +46% across human single-joint studies, ×1.1–1.8 in animal tissue**, **+19% over 300 ms in an isokinetic squat**, of which most is also given by an **isometric preload** (+14%), which needs no elastic element. The gain decays with delay (half-life 0.85 s in one lifting study; faster in others, unread). The excess to be covered at the trunk is **15–53%** over the 288 bound (344/288 = 1.19), at the joints' peak torque and not their work. The measured percentages are of the same order, **but no source measures them for trunk rotation**, and a gain in *work* over a window is not a gain in *peak torque*.
- **Representation.** Torque-driven models that add elasticity put a spring **in series with the contractile element at each joint, stiffness set so the stretch is 4–5% at maximum isometric torque** (K from a few hundred to 5,000 N·m/rad; stored energy at maximum torque 20–60 J per leg joint, my arithmetic). That is a tendon-based store; **no trunk value exists.** A trunk element would need its own stiffness and rest angle, with nothing measured to set it (the passive axial stiffness papers were seen as abstracts only).
- **Evidence for the trunk store itself.** Correlations of separation (X-factor stretch) with speed are mixed or null; one softball sample found a negative correlation (r = −0.35); the mechanism is "not understood" in the reviews. The kinematic fact that the pelvis–thorax twist builds and returns within about 0.2 s is observed in two batters. **Nothing measured shows stored elastic energy of the trunk or hips in joules.**

## 6. Not found

## 1. Shoulder and elbow torques (inverse dynamics)

All from the American Sports Medicine Institute (ASMI) method: 12 cameras at 240 Hz, ~47 markers, 4th-order Butterworth filter at 13.4 Hz, ball a 142 g point mass until release; torque is the net torque at the joint by the more proximal segment on the more distal one, "near the instant of maximal shoulder external rotation" or "near ball release".

**Where the sources disagree.** Attias's shoulder numbers (about 350 N·m peak) are three to four times the ASMI "instant of maximal external rotation" values (100–120 N·m; 91–100 in the other two). The ASMI figures are the net torque about a defined axis **at one instant**, in a joint frame; Attias's are peaks of a different (Visual3D, OpenSim joint-frame) torque on a single pitcher, possibly over the whole cycle and about different axes. I could not reconcile the definitions. Any design that needs a shoulder torque cap should take it from the ASMI definition if its model's joint axes match theirs, and treat Attias's as an actuator limit only. ASMI's 1995 values (67, 64 N·m) are 30–35 % below its own 2025 values at similar level (and the 2025 pitchers are heavier: 95 kg).

## 3. Sequence and timing of the delivery

- **Timing of peaks as a fraction of the interval from lead-foot contact (0 %) to release (100 %)** (Escamilla 2025, **FT**, 18 college pitchers, dirt mound): peak pelvis angular velocity at **24 ± 8 %** (569 ± 73 °/s = 9.9 rad/s), peak upper-trunk angular velocity at **46 ± 8 %** (1096 ± 60 °/s = 19.1 rad/s). Fleisig 2025 (standard ball): pelvis 565 °/s, upper trunk 1173 ± 17 (SE) °/s, **peak elbow extension velocity 2790 °/s (48.7 rad/s)**, **peak shoulder internal-rotation velocity 6718 °/s (117 rad/s)**, elbow flexion at release 27°, trunk forward tilt at release 31.5°, stride length 82.5 % of height, arm slot 54.7° from vertical.
- **Absolute duration.** One secondary description puts foot contact to release at **about 0.145 s** (**2nd**, a review that I did not open; population not stated). Attias's model starts at peak lead-knee height and runs **0.7 s** to release (measured mean, **FT**). Fleisig's 1999 comparison found **no difference in the six timing parameters across levels** (youth to professional; **AB**), so the same timing pattern is reported for ages 10 to professionals.
- **Kinetic chain / where the work comes from.** Roach & Lieberman 2014 (J Exp Biol; **AB**): inverse dynamics on 20 baseball players wearing braces that block specific joint motions: "most of the work produced during throwing is generated at the **hips**", used to load elastic elements in the shoulder and power the arm; the elbow and wrist's high velocities are powered by proximal segments; wrist hyperextension adds "only modestly". Roach et al. 2013 (Nature 498:483; **AB**): the shoulder stores and releases elastic energy (humeral torsion, a low-slung shoulder, a mobile waist; their evidence is from throwing experiments). **Elastic storage at the shoulder is therefore the carrier of the late, very fast internal rotation**, and it is a property of the passive tissue, not a drive. Hirashima 2008 (note 1): elbow extension and wrist flexion are accelerated by velocity-dependent torque from the trunk and upper arm.
- **Lower body and ball speed.** Kageyama 2014 above: higher-velocity pitchers use greater pivot-leg (hip, knee) torques before foot contact and greater GRF and stride-knee extension after it; MacWilliams 1998: drive-leg force correlates with wrist velocity. Stodden et al. 2005 (J Appl Biomech 21:44; **AB**, title only found: "within pitcher variation").

## 4. Release: timing precision and the fingers

- **Timing windows.** Jegede et al. 2005 (J Sports Sci 23:805; **AB**): 9 unskilled and 6 skilled throwers, 30 fast accurate overarm throws each, search coils at 1000 Hz, radar for speed. Mean **timing windows for ball release: 28 ms (unskilled), 7 ms (skilled)**; ball speed depended on release timing in the unskilled only; in skilled throwers the variability of ball speed followed variability of **peak forearm angular velocity**, i.e. arm speed, not release timing.
- **How the release time is held.** Hore & Watts 2011 (J Neurophysiol 106:2024; **AB**): skilled throwers release "on the handpath with a timing precision as low as **1 ms**"; with no ball in the hand they cannot open the fingers rapidly or time the opening to 1–2 ms. The authors conclude the precision comes from the **back-force of the ball pushing the fingers open** (Newtonian), with the brain commanding only the initial opening, and finger stiffness chosen from an estimate of hand acceleration. For the game: release time is set by the hand's acceleration and finger stiffness, not by a timer.
- **Finger forces.** Kinoshita et al. 2017 (Hum Mov Sci 54:172; **AB**): 11 pitchers, an instrumented baseball with one tri-axial force transducer under one fingertip at a time, four-seam fastballs from a flat mound. Index and middle fingers: bimodal force, peaks **38–39 ms and 6–7 ms before release**, each about **97 N**; ring finger about **50 N**; thumb about **83 N**; shear forces of the index and middle fingers peak **4–5 ms before release** and sum to **102 N** ("a kinetic source for backspin"); with submaximal effort the peak force fell linearly with ball velocity; the peak was over **80 % of maximum finger strength** measured. Matsuo et al. are co-authors; I did not find a separate Matsuo paper to read.
- **Spin axis.** Jinji et al. 2011 (J Sports Sci 29:761; **AB**): 19 pitchers, 1000 Hz capture; the spin axis follows the **hand's orientation just before release**, the ball rotating in the plane formed by the palm and fingers.

## 5. Source differences to carry

- Lab fastballs run 3–7 mph below game speed (Fleisig 2025, anecdotal); Kageyama's high group (37.4 m/s = 83.7 mph) matches the professional group of the 1999 ASMI study (37.0 m/s, quoted in Kageyama's discussion).
- Collegiate pitchers' typical lab speeds: 33–35 m/s (Kageyama, quoting Feltner & Dapena 1986; Fleisig 1999; Sakurai 1993; Stodden 2001).
- ASMI's elbow-varus numbers rose from 64 N·m (1995, 26 pitchers) to 92–100 N·m (2025, 18–20 college and AA pitchers); the groups and the filtering differ, so do not treat 1995's value as a current professional norm.

## 6. What this gives the design

1. **Shoulder and elbow torques are published numbers** (ASMI: internal rotation 90–100 N·m, varus 90–100 N·m, horizontal adduction 90–120 N·m, elbow flexion 49–73 N·m, at college/AA level, ~85 mph in the lab). They are net torques at one instant, so they are limits to check a model against, not drive profiles.
2. **The whole-body delivery has the same timing pattern at every level**, which supports one delivery shape scaled by strength; the pelvis peaks at about a quarter and the trunk at about half of the foot-contact-to-release interval.
3. **The elbow and wrist are driven by the body, not by their own muscles** (Hirashima 2008, Roach 2014): a driven-torso, shoulder-spring model is consistent with the evidence; the shoulder spring (elastic storage) has to be modelled or the 117 rad/s internal rotation cannot be reached.
4. **Release is a hand-and-finger event** with ~1–7 ms timing windows and finger forces of 50–100 N each, ending 4–7 ms before release; it follows the hand's acceleration, so an error in the arm becomes a release error without a separate "release timer".
5. **Ground forces for a pitch are only partly numeric here** (0.35 BW drive shear, 0.72 BW landing shear, MacWilliams 1998); the forces themselves have to be found elsewhere or derived from the model's own mass and CoM motion.

# 10. Smoothed sources: what in the project copies a motion that a source smoothed (PLAN D35)

Written 2026-11-25 by the worker `smooth-audit` on `work/smoothing-audit` (from main `65be0c2`). Docs only: no code, spec, fit or calibration changed. The owner's instruction after D35: "Make sure we aren't trying to mimic any other motion that is based on smoothed data."

**Revised the same day after merging main** (`67fb690`: the session 7 review's fixes; body-motion §4.4's "For the owner" and §4.8 rewritten; session 7's "2,649–2,841°/s" and "27.6–29.9 m/s" withdrawn, review P1-1 and P1-2). Every figure this note had taken from the old §4.8 is redone on the new one's range across shapes. **§1a says where this note agrees with the new §4.8 and where it still differs, with the evidence.**

Evidence levels as in the other notes: **FT** full text read, **TAB** a table of it, **FIG** read off a figure, **AB** abstract only, **EXC** an excerpt or vendor page, **derived** computed here from measured numbers by the stated method, **[E]** assumed. Every size below that says "derived" is an estimate from a stated shape passed through a stated filter, not a measurement.

**The probe.** `sim/target/codex-probes/smoothing-audit/probe.py` (scipy through `uv run --with scipy --with numpy python probe.py`; 20 s; output `probe.out` beside it; gitignored, like every probe there). It filters *positions* (an angle, or a marker's coordinates) and differentiates them with a 3-point central difference, as [4] did ([4] §3.4: "A three-point central difference method was used to calculate global velocities", FT). [4] states only "a 12 Hz Butterworth filter", so four variants are run [E]: **A** 2nd order forward and back (a 4th-order zero-phase filter, −6 dB at 12 Hz); **B** 4th order forward and back (8th-order zero-phase); **C** as A with the two-pass correction (−3 dB at 12 Hz); **D** 4th order in one pass (not zero-phase). `batspeed.py` (body-motion §4.8) was rerun beside it with `BB_FORT_CSV` pointing at the digitised curves; its output is `batspeed.out` there (before the merge) and `batspeed-merged.out` (after). `window.out` there is a short script over the merged `batspeed.fit_shapes()`: for every shape §4.8 says fits, the true rate against [4]'s filtered curve from −70 ms to contact (§1a.3).

## 1a. Against body-motion §4.8 and §4.4 as rewritten (after the merge)

1. **Figures from the old §4.8, redone.** The old straight-piece rates (2,649–2,841°/s just before contact) are withdrawn by the review (P1-2: the pieces jumped at the window's edge). On the new §4.8's shapes that fit (accelerating rises joined to [4]'s curve at ±70 ms, the step at most a free bat's; **the shape [E]**), the bat turns at **2,970–3,540°/s** just before contact. Against that: [4]'s 2,217 at contact is **63–75%** of the true rate (the first version said "about 20% slow"; it is 25–37% slow); swing v3's ratios before the last 70 ms over-read by **1.34–1.60** (was 1.20–1.28); the cock undone reads 57.9–60.5° (4.0–6.6° less than 64.5); the bat is 4–6° further round and meets −8° 1–2 ms earlier. All four depend on the shape; none is a measurement.
2. **The angles at contact.** The first version said to read the lag and elevation at contact "as the unsmoothed values". Withdrawn: the elevation undone has the barrel chopping down at 19–22° (review P1-1), and D35 has the body not matched inside the last 40 ms at all but checked there against hitters measured directly. §6 item 2 is rewritten to say so.
3. **The window: the new §4.8 and D35 (about 40 ms) are right for what the next fit leaves out; this note's 20–25 ms was too short.** The two numbers measure different things. 20–23 ms is where a *bare step* through [4]'s filter first shows 5% of itself (§3.1). But the bat's true rate is not flat before the step: on every shape that fits it rises steeply over the last 70 ms, and the filter spreads that steepening too, back to about −50 ms. On those shapes the true rate differs from [4]'s curve by up to 0.3 band units at −50 ms, 0.4 at −40, 0.5–0.6 at −30 and −25, and more from −15 ms on (`window.out`; derived, the shape [E]). Outside 70 ms the difference is zero by construction, so "70 ms" is the assumption's edge, not a finding. **For the next fit: leave out the bat's rate, elevation and lag from −40 ms to contact**; from −70 to −40 ms the curve is within 0.4 band units of every fitting shape, so keeping it costs at most that. The body's own curves (pelvis, trunk, knees, elbows) have no step and no steep rise at contact, and the 20–25 ms of a step is the right reach for anything the impact itself could jolt there.
4. **The hands at contact: this note differs from the new §4.8 on one point, the impact's direction.** §4.8 (`batspeed.py` `hands_through_filter`) takes the impact as taking speed *off* the hands ("the handle of a free bat is kicked 3.3 m/s"; "a loss of the hands' speed") and so lets the filter account for 0.9–1.7 m/s of the hands' slowing at contact. `batspeed.py` computes the handle's change as a magnitude (`P.norm`), not its sign. With its own collision code (the same bat, ball and restitutions) the handle's velocity *along the swing* changes by **+3.3 m/s at the mid-grip, +4.6 at the lead grip, +2.0 at the rear grip**, while the sweet spot loses 12.2 (`sign.py` beside the probe, which reruns `batspeed.collision()`'s contact and keeps the sign; derived). The handle is pushed forward because the sweet spot (0.714 m from the knob) is 3–4 cm beyond the bat's centre of percussion about the hands (0.674–0.682 m). So the impact, spread back by the filter, would make the hands look slightly *faster* before contact, not slower; it cannot explain the slowing from 11.0 to 6.5 m/s. Where I agree with §4.8: the slowing from 11.0 to about 8 m/s by −10 ms is in [4]'s curves before the filter reaches them; and the hands are placed on the bat's smoothed angles, so the smoothing reaches them that way (with the azimuth undone the placement's hands lose their component along the sweet spot's path). That route is real, but it is an inconsistency of mixing undone and smoothed curves along an [E] hand path, not a measured slowing. **So the hands' 6.5 m/s at contact is not shown to be a smoothing effect, and nothing direct says real hands are at 6.5 at contact; the fitted body's 3.3 is half of an unmeasured figure.** Suggested fix in `batspeed.py`: report the handle's change along the sweet spot's direction, with its sign.
5. **[OB]'s "98% at contact" and "2 ± 5 ms", and §4.4's and D35's "hitters measured directly lose about 2%".** What is known of [OB]: "Marker-derived signals were filtered with a 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter at 20 Hz", and kinematics "are computed … then filtered" (datasheet, FT); "Contact and marker-derived bat metrics are exploratory estimates; the illustrated ball flight is not measured" (hitting README, FT); the events provided are "front foot contact (10% bodyweight), front foot plant (100% bodyweight), and contact", with no definition of contact; the released code reads `e.contact_time` from a database, so how contact is found is not released. No ball is tracked. The 98% is the ratio of two means (66.2 mph at contact, 67.1 at the fastest); the note-writer's own differencing gave 94.5% with the peak 2 ± 5 ms early ([note 6](../swing/6-openbiomechanics.md)). Through [OB]'s own filter a real impact at the stated contact would read about 90% with the peak 11–14 ms early (§3.3). **So the 2% bears almost no weight**: [OB]'s contact is probably set at or before the impact's filtered dip, and then 98% is close to what a filtered peak gives whether or not the bat slows. What does bear weight that a bat does not slow before contact:
   - *The collision's physics.* Nothing touches the bat before the ball; the loss arrives within the 0.6–1.0 ms of contact. The 9% that [4]'s curve loses before contact is what its stated filter does to that loss (§3.1; §4.8). This explains [4]'s shape; by itself it does not forbid a hitter whose hands brake the bat before contact.
   - *Statcast's bat speed against where the ball is met* (agent-models §3.6, τ_b): balls met further out front, later in the same kind of swing, are met at higher bat speed, out to 6–12 in in front of a batter's mean. At the usual contact point the bat is still speeding up. This holds whatever Statcast's processing is, if it is the same at every depth (not published).
   - *The exit-velocity floor* (§4.8): [4]'s own batted balls need 23.1–24.7 m/s at the sweet spot, more than its smoothed 22.9 at contact. That shows the smoothed contact value is too low, not when the bat peaked.
   - *Sensors.* Blast reads [OB]'s swings faster at contact (30.5 m/s) than [OB]'s markers do at their fastest (30.0), consistent with the markers being smoothed. Blast's processing is not stated (EXC).
   - Not found: an instrumented-bat or high-rate unfiltered measurement of a bat's speed through the last 20 ms before impact. The nearest would be [OB]'s raw C3D files differenced without a filter across contact (non-commercial use, D21).

   Suggested wording for §4.4 and D35's rationale: "a real bat is still speeding up when it meets the ball (Statcast by contact depth; the collision's physics)", without the 2%.

## 3. What the filters do where we read them (derived)

### 3.1 A sudden loss at contact

The bat loses about a third of its turning in a millisecond when it meets the ball (body-motion §4.8: a free bat would lose 1,519°/s; [4]'s own curve, refitted, 794–1,073). Through a zero-phase filter the loss arrives early. Share of the loss already showing, by the filter (probe §1):

| | −30 ms | −20 | −15 | −10 | −5 | 0 | 5% of the loss first shows | angle's error at contact |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: | --: |
| [4], 12 Hz at 300 Hz (A, B, C) | −2 to +1% | 5–11% | 14–20% | 25–30% | 39–42% | 54–55% | −20 to −23 ms | −3.0 to −3.9 ms × the loss |
| [3], 13.3 Hz at 200 Hz | −3 to 0% | 4–10% | 13–19% | 26–30% | 41–43% | 57–59% | −15 to −20 ms | −2.3 to −3.1 ms × the loss |
| [OB], 20 Hz at 360 Hz | | −3 to +1% | 1–6% | 12–18% | 32–36% | 56–57% | −11 to −14 ms | −1.6 to −2.2 ms × the loss |
| one pass (D), any | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | after contact, 8–17 ms late | −19 to −33 ms × the loss |

- **A bare step first shows (5% of itself) 20–23 ms before contact through [4]'s filter.** That is the reach of anything the impact itself jolts. It is *not* the window to leave out of the bat's curves: there the true rate also rises steeply before the step, and the filter spreads that too, so the bat's curves are off from about −50 ms (§1a.3: leave out 40 ms, as D35 says). A zero-phase filter also rings: at −40 to −30 ms the rate reads 2–7% of the loss *above* the true one.
- **[4]'s filter was zero-phase, or acts as one.** A one-pass filter would leave the rate untouched before contact and delay it after; [4]'s curve falls before contact (2,419°/s at −10 ms, 2,217 at contact) and is about half-way at contact, as the zero-phase variants are. (Derived; the order stays [E].)
- **An angle at contact is off by the loss times 2–4 ms**: 1,000°/s of loss puts the bat's azimuth 3–4° behind at contact, which is §4.8's lag and elevation result from another direction.

### 3.2 A peak of a given width

Kept share of a Gaussian rate peak through [4]'s filter, by its true full width at half height (probe §2): 20 ms 48–58%; 30 ms 66–74%; 40 ms 77–84%; 60 ms 90–94%; 80 ms 95–98%; 100 ms 98–99%; 150 ms over 99%.

[4]'s own peaks, already filtered, are broad: the pelvis's 157 ms at half height, the trunk's 124, the lead knee's extension 135, the rear elbow's 109 (from the angle curves' slopes). **So the 12 Hz filter takes under 2% off any body segment's rate peak in [4]**; the trunk 0.4–1.6%. What blunts the body's peaks is the averaging (§4). The one narrow peak, the bat's, is made by the impact.

For the pitcher (§11): a shoulder's internal rotation rate rising to its peak near release and falling in a few tens of ms is in the 20–40 ms class, where 13.4 Hz keeps half to four fifths. How narrow ASMI's peaks are was not read [E], so for the delivery this is a warning, not a size.

### 3.3 A marker on a fast-turning bat, and the bat's speed

A filter run on a marker's coordinates shrinks a curved path: a point going round at 2,841°/s (7.9 Hz; the old §4.8's straight-piece rate, used as the probe's input: at the new §4.8's 2,970–3,540°/s, 8.3–9.8 Hz, the shrink in steady rotation is larger, 69–82% of the radius through A and 84–92% through C) keeps 1/(1 + (7.9/12)⁴) = 84% of its circle's radius through filter A at 12 Hz. Speeds taken from filtered marker paths are low even with no impact; **angles between two markers are not** (both shrink together). Probe §4: a marker 0.75 m from fixed hands [E], the bat's true rate from §4.8's straight-piece fit (1,830°/s at −40 ms, 2,841 just before contact, a loss of 1,073 at contact) joined to [4]'s curve at ±70 ms. (This reconstruction reads 190°/s under [4]'s curve at −10 ms when filtered; the ratios below are the result, not the absolute speeds.)

| The bat's end, speed from the filtered marker | [4] 12 Hz | [3] 13.3 Hz | [OB] 20 Hz |
| :-- | --: | --: | --: |
| no impact, at contact, of the true speed (A; B and C) | 81%; 89% | 85%; 91% | 94%; 96% |
| with the impact, at contact | 67–71% | 68–71% | 74–75% |
| with the impact, the filtered speed's peak | 13–17 ms before contact | 15 ms before | 11–14 ms before |
| the azimuth rate from the same filtered markers against the angle filtered directly | within 40°/s | within 40°/s | within 20°/s |

- **[4] is not touched by this**: it reports angles and their rates, and the placed motion's speeds are made from angles.
- **[3]'s bat-end speeds are.** Its 31 m/s (fastest, −15 ms) and 29 (at contact) are from its end marker's filtered path: low by 9–15% from the path's curvature alone [derived; the order of "4th-order zero-phase" decides between 85 and 91%], so at least 34–36 m/s at the end before any impact correction, and the impact's spread takes more at contact. Its peak at −15 ms is the filter's, as [4]'s is. The gate's "29" (body-motion §13) and swing-model-v3's tip-speed checks inherit both.
- **[OB]'s bat speeds are low by 4–6% from the curvature at 20 Hz** if they come from filtered marker paths or filtered velocity components (both are filtered, per its datasheet), before any impact effect. Blast's sensor on the same swings reads 30.5 m/s against the markers' 29.6 at contact, 3% higher (Blast's own processing not stated).
- **[OB]'s 98% at contact.** Through its own 20 Hz filter a real impact would leave the bat's speed at contact near 90% of the filtered peak and the peak 11–14 ms early. [OB] reports 98% and 2 ± 5 ms. So its "contact", an "exploratory estimate" without a tracked ball, is probably not the impact's instant (it may sit at or before the speed's top). What can and cannot rest on it, and the other evidence that a bat does not slow before contact: §1a.5.

### 3.4 The hands (the owner's named case; body-3d-7fix has the detail)

My own estimate (reconciled with the new §4.8 in §1a.4). **The impact cannot have slowed the hands**: on the game's bat (physics-spec §6.2: 0.965 kg, centre of mass 0.597 m from the knob, 0.0382 kg·m² about it) the centre of percussion about the hands is 0.674–0.682 m from the knob and the sweet spot 0.714 m, beyond it, so a free bat's handle is pushed **forward** by the ball (+3.3 m/s at the mid-grip on `batspeed.py`'s own collision), never slowed; held, less; and [4] reports no hand path, so none of that reached the placement directly. **The smoothing does reach the placed hands through the bat's smoothed angles** they are closed onto, by an amount that depends on the [E] hand path and on mixing undone and smoothed curves (§4.8: with the azimuth undone their component along the sweet spot's path goes from +3.2 to −0.9 to −1.8 m/s). So 6.5 m/s at contact is neither a measurement nor a size the smoothing explains. **11.0 m/s at the hands' fastest (−50 ms) is outside the impact's reach.** The filter takes 1–3% off a hand-speed peak 80–100 ms wide (probe §5; the placed curve falls from 11.0 at −50 to 9.5 at −20 and 6.5 at contact, so it is at least that wide). The bigger and unknown part is averaging: the arm's own rate peaks are 8% (lead shoulder's azimuth) and 11% (rear elbow's extension) over [4]'s mean curves (§4), and the hands are their sum, so a single batter's hand peak is plausibly 5–10% over the mean motion's [E]: 11.5–12 m/s, inside Blast's 10.3–13.0 (whose own processing is not stated). The fitted body's 7.6 m/s at its fastest against Blast's 10.3–13.0 has nothing to do with the smoothing (as §4.4 says).

## 5. Today's game

- **The swing (v2, `sim/crates/bb_agents/src/swing.rs`; agent-models §3.6)** is fitted to Statcast (the sweet spot's speed at contact, swing length, attack angle and direction at the frame nearest the ball, swing path tilt over the 40 ms before contact, contact depth, the within-batter SD of bat speed) and Blast (time to contact 0.149 s, the vertical bat angle). None of its shapes copy [3], [4] or [OB]: the two Gaussian bumps, τ_b = +10 ms (the bat still speeding up at contact) and q are [E]. **Statcast's processing is the open item.** If its velocity at the frame nearest the ball comes from a model fitted across the impact, its bat speeds carry some of the same loss; if from frames before the impact, none. Not published (collision-targets §4 searched; this search found only "five [cameras] running at 300 frames per second" and the 90% rule). At 300 frames a second a difference over one frame either side of contact would straddle the impact.
- **Running**: Statcast's sprint speed is the best one-second window of a run; for v = v_max(1 − e^(−t/τ)) at τ = 0.76 s the window reads under 1% low (runner-acceleration note §2 already put the asymptote at about 8.25 against the sim's 8.23 m/s). No sudden event inside.
- **Throws, pitches and batted balls** are matched to tracked flights, which have no sudden event; not D35's concern.
- **Nothing else in the sim crates is fitted to a recorded body curve** (`grep` of `sim/crates` for Fortenbaugh, Welch, Appendix E: only `bb_game/examples/swing_contact_baseline.rs`, a measuring example that uses [4]'s azimuth convention).

So **nothing in the game as it plays today is fitted to a smoothed stretch that is known**; the one unknown is how Statcast makes a bat speed.

1. **Swing v3, before its build step 4 (the game as it will play).** `analysis/oracle/swing_v3.py` divides [4]'s rate curve by its smoothed contact value (`RATE4_RATIO`, line 197, over `RATE4_DPS[-1]` = 2,214°/s); `I_HAT`, `I_93` and `RATE_93` (0.558) follow. ## 7. For the owner

**What we were copying that was not real.** Two studies we use filtered their camera data over the whole swing, straight through the moment the bat meets the ball. That makes the bat look as if it starts slowing before contact; in fact it is still speeding up when the ball arrives. The force-driven body was being fitted to the last 40 ms of that (its bat's turning, how far the barrel has dropped, how much the wrists are still cocked, and exactly when it meets the ball); you have ruled that out (D35). The new swing written for the game (v3, not yet in the game) copies it more widely: it measures the whole swing's speed against the study's slowed-down contact speed, so its bat turns a third to 60% too fast early in the swing (on the bat paths that fit the study's curve) and then eases off into the ball.

**How big.** How fast the bat really turned cannot be read back out exactly; on the bat paths that reproduce the study's curve, at contact the study reads the bat's turning at 63–75% of the real rate, the bat 4–6° behind where it really was, the wrists 4–7° too cocked, and the contact 1–2 ms late. Undoing the barrel's drop gives a swing that chops down on the ball, so that one is not real either way. The older tee study's bat-end speeds (where the "29 m/s" came from) are low twice over: by the same impact effect, and by another 9–15% that its filter takes off any marker going round fast. The college lab we used as a check filters too (less), and how it times contact is unpublished, so its "the bat is at 98% at contact" is not proof of anything.

**Averaging.** The study's curves are averages of 33 hitters who did not peak at the same moment, so no hitter's peak is in them: the front foot's peak push is 130% of body weight on the average curve where the hitters themselves averaged 149%; the trunk's turning 6% lower; the knee's straightening 16% lower. The body is being fitted to a blurred average batter. This is not a filter, but it is the same kind of thing.

**What is clean.** For the bat, everything more than about 40 ms before contact; for the body, everything more than about 25 ms before it: the hips' and trunk's turning (the filter takes under 2% off them), the stride, the knees and elbows, the shoulders, the bat's first move, how the body starts. The hands: the ball cannot have slowed them (it pushes the handle forward), but their 6.5 m/s at contact is built from the study's smoothed bat angles along a hand path we had to assume, so it is not a measurement either; their top speed (11 m/s) is a little low, mostly from averaging. The college lab's "bat at 98% of its fastest at contact" proves little (its contact moment is an unpublished estimate); that a real bat is still speeding up at contact rests on the physics and on Statcast's faster swings for balls met further out front. The game as it plays today copies no smoothed motion: its swing is fitted to Statcast and its running to Statcast sprint speed (under 1% off). The one thing nobody has published is how Statcast itself turns its camera frames into a bat speed.

**Waiting on you:** nothing new. Q59 and D35 already cover the body's bat targets; items 1 and 3 of §6 are choices for the next swing and body sessions. One wording in D35's reason ("hitters measured directly lose about 2%") leans on the college lab's figure; §1a.5 suggests a firmer one.

## 8. Not established, and what would settle it

- **[4]'s filter order and the two-pass correction, and the bat's true shape before contact**: the sizes move by about a fifth between variants. [4]'s unfiltered marker paths, or Motion Analysis's EVaRT/Cortex documentation for 2010, would settle it.
- **Statcast's bat speed**: MLB's methodology (a frame difference or a model fit, through the impact or not).
- **[OB]'s contact event**: its code or authors; or recomputing from its raw C3D files (non-commercial use only, D21), which would also give single-batter peaks unfiltered by averaging.
- **The hands' single-batter peak**: any set with hand markers and per-swing peaks (Blast's is a sensor; its processing is not stated).
- **The pitcher's arm rates near release** at 13.4 Hz against a higher cut-off: no study comparing cut-offs on the same deliveries was found in one search.
- **Not reached**: the fielders' Statcast "jump" and burst (no motion model in the sim copies them), the catcher's pop time (derived in the sim, not fitted), and Kinoshita's finger forces (sensor processing not read).

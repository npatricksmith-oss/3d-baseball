# Body research 1: forward-dynamics models of striking and throwing driven by joint torque generators (2026-11-25, worker body-research)

For [PLAN D31 / §14.1](../../../PLAN.md): the precedents for a body whose joints are driven by a few torque generators, with the bat's path or the ball's release as a result. Reading status is stated on every source: **FT** = full text read (PDF or full-text extract), **AB** = abstract or catalogue record only, **2nd** = a later paper's or a search summary's description.

### 1.2 Cricket fast bowling: the closest precedent for a throw with legs and ground (Felton, Yeadon, King)

Felton, Shine, Yeadon, King 2023, J Biomech, DOI 10.1016/j.jbiomech.2023.111765, CC BY. **FT.** Earlier versions: Felton, Yeadon, King 2020 J Sports Sci (**AB**/**2nd**: 4.0 % RMS difference to the evaluated performance, 3.5 m/s faster optimised release), and later strength-study and ISBS versions (**2nd**, see the last bullet of this section).

- **Model.** Planar, **16 segments**: 14 rigid (head+trunk, two upper arms, two thighs, two shanks, two two-part feet, non-bowling forearm+hand, bowling forearm, bowling hand), plus two *massless* segments (pelvis, shoulder girdle) whose length and angle follow a third-order Fourier series of trunk angle, so the hip and shoulder centres need not coincide. "Wobbling masses" (non-linear spring-dampers) in the shank, thigh and trunk. The front foot meets the ground through horizontal and vertical linear spring-dampers at three points (heel, metatarsophalangeal joint, toe). The ball is a point mass on a viscoelastic spring at the hand so release is smooth.
- **Generators.** Flexor and extensor torque generators, each a contractile plus series-elastic component (King et al. 2006), at both shoulders, both hips, and on the front leg the knee, ankle and toe joint, and on the bowling arm the elbow and wrist. The rear leg's toe, ankle and knee and the non-bowling elbow are **angle-driven** (prescribed from the measurement) because "no established links" to speed. So the model is a hybrid: few driven joints, the rest played back.
- **Activation.** Each generator's activation follows a **quintic** function with zero velocity and acceleration at its ends (Yeadon & Hiley 2000), with a **minimum ramp time of 70 ms** (Yeadon et al. 2010), in one of four patterns (up–up, up–down, down–up, down–down). 88 of the fit's parameters are these timings.
- **Generator strength.** A **nine-parameter** maximum-torque function of joint angle and angular velocity (Yeadon, King & Wilson 2006: a seven-parameter torque–velocity part times a two-parameter quadratic torque–angle part, **2nd**) fitted to **isovelocity dynamometer data** (Con-Trex MJ) for the ankle, knee, hip, shoulder and wrist, and scaled to the individual (King et al. 2009). The toe, bowling elbow and bowling shoulder could not be tested on the dynamometer and were given a **constant** net torque.
- **Fit to one performance (evaluation).** Ten elite bowlers (age 20.7 ± 2.4 y, 1.91 ± 0.08 m, 86.9 ± 8.5 kg), 300 Hz motion capture, 1800 Hz force plate; the best of 12 deliveries per bowler. **107 parameters** varied by genetic algorithm: 8 passive-torque, 10 strength scalings, **88 activation timings**, 1 release timing. Result: mean objective difference **5.0 %** (SD 0.8; range 4.0–6.5), of which kinematic **1.9 %** (0.9–2.7) and force **11.3 %** (9.2–15.0). The force match is the weak part.
- **What it did with the model.** Optimising the initial position and 98 activation parameters (seven joints) raised release speed from 35.9 ± 1.5 to 40.7 ± 1.6 m/s (+4.8 ± 1.3 m/s, 13.5 %) with the contact phase unchanged at ~101 ms and **lower** peak ground forces. Common to all ten: a more extended front knee, and **delayed** onset of trunk flexion and of arm circumduction. The model predicts what a *better* technique would be; it was not built to predict one bowler's next delivery.
- **2nd (search summary, not opened):** an earlier ISBS version on one bowler found technique changes worth +10 % (timings) and +22 % (initial position), and **+5 % strength gave only +1 % ball speed**: that bowler was technique-limited, not strength-limited.

### 1.5 Timing-optimal throwing (the principle, not the sports model)

- Alexander 1991, "Optimum timing of muscle activation for simple models of throwing", J Theor Biol 150:349–372. **AB**/catalogue only (note: it is *J Theor Biol*, not J Biomech): muscles fire in sequence; there is an optimum delay between the proximal and the distal one that maximises release speed.
- Herring & Chapman 1992, J Biomech 25:1173–1184. **2nd**: planar three-link throw; rapid torque reversal at proximal joints improved speed, but a later critique (Kojima) found the reversal rate used was unrealistically fast and a physiologically plausible reversal gave no meaningful gain.
- Sprigings & Neal 2000 and MacKenzie & Sprigings 2009 above reach the same proximal-to-distal result for the golf downswing; Felton's bowling optimum *delays* the arm.

## 2. How few muscle groups have such models needed?

| Task | Generators that were driven | Reproduced |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Golf downswing, 2-D (Sprigings & Neal 2000) | **3** (torso, shoulder, wrist), 6 control variables | clubhead speed 44 m/s, timing in agreement with a pro's video qualitatively only |
| Golf downswing, 3-D (MacKenzie & Sprigings 2009) | **4**, 10 control variables | one golfer's segment angles to 0.7–1.5° and clubhead speed to 1.3 m/s RMS (R² > 0.99), **no legs** |
| Cricket bowling front-foot phase (Felton 2023) | about 9 joints × flexor + extensor, 88 timing parameters; the rest of the body prescribed | kinematics 1.9 %, forces 11.3 % of the objective, ten bowlers |
| Baseball lower body (tee) | 17 generators over 7 segments, 388 matching parameters | angles within 2–4 % RMS, after *fitting torque values within ±5 % of the inverse-dynamics ones* |

So: **three to four lumped generators** have been enough to reproduce a golf swing's kinematics to about 1° and its clubhead speed to ~1 m/s; **about two dozen** were used for a whole-body throw with ground contact; no published model of a baseball swing or pitch with few generators was found. Every one of the fits still gives each generator its own **onset time and duration** (and sometimes a scaling) as free parameters found by a global search against the measurement.

## 3. How they kept a model on its path

- **Open-loop activation timing found by optimisation** (all of the sports models above): there is no feedback. The match is a *fit* of 6 to 107 timings to one performance, done after the fact. A prediction for a new situation is a new optimisation.
- **Prescribed joints** (Felton: rear leg and non-bowling elbow angle-driven; Koike: the feet's centre of pressure and some torques from measurement; Attias: tracking step first, then the predictive step starting from the tracking solution).
- **Penalties and passive limit torques** keep joints inside their range and the end position right (Sprigings & Neal: shaft within ±0.5° of vertical; Koike: angles within 10 % of measured; Felton: anatomical limits and no excess wobbling mass motion).
- **Short horizons.** Golf downswings 0.34–0.38 s, tee swing about 0.20 s, bowling front-foot phase ~0.10 s, pitching 0.7 s (Attias; with the optimiser given the measured motion as its first guess). None integrated 1.5 s of delivery open-loop; the 0.7 s one needed Moco's collocation, not a forward integration.

## 4. What this gives the design

1. A **few lumped generators with an onset time, a duration and a strength cap** is the established form (golf: 3–4; whole-body throws: ~20), with the activation as a first-order rise (τ 20–40 ms) or a smooth ramp (quintic, ≥ 70 ms) and a torque–angular-velocity fall-off. The numbers for golf are in §1.1.
2. **Timing, not strength, is what the optimisations move** (Koike: +25 % trunk speed from timing alone; Felton's bowler: +10 % from timing, +1 % from +5 % strength). The sequence is proximal to distal, with large late torques that only *hold* speed.
3. Every precedent **fits timings to a measured swing** with a global search; none runs a controller that produces the motion for a new pitch. D31's "no motion fitted to its planned end" has **no direct precedent** in the sports literature; the nearest are the "learned swing" idea (note 6, timing variability) and open-loop timing sets.
4. The agreement to expect from a fitted few-generator model is **1–4 % RMS in angles and ~1 m/s in end speed**; ground-force agreement is poorer (~11 % in cricket).
5. Models that dropped the legs (golf) overstate the generators; ground contact modelled as spring-dampers (cricket) is workable at 100 ms; no precedent handles a 1.5 s delivery in one open-loop run.
